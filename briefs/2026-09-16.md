## 1. AI INDUSTRY

### OpenAI confirms it has been coordinating with Anthropic and Google DeepMind on AI safety

OpenAI global policy chief Chris Lehane said Tuesday, September 15, that the company's engagement with Anthropic and Google DeepMind on safety measures had been under way for several weeks. Lehane said OpenAI does not believe the three firms need an antitrust waiver to coordinate, arguing that existing law already permits safety-related information sharing, as it does among airlines. The Information reported the three have discussed forming a shared AI safety standards body. Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei has asked the US government to "mediate or at least enable" the cooperation through a standards body or explicit waivers.

**In plain terms:** These are the three companies building the most capable AI systems in the United States, and they normally compete hard against each other. Competitors agreeing among themselves on how they will all behave is the exact thing antitrust law exists to police, because that kind of agreement can slide into fixing prices or dividing up a market. OpenAI's argument is that safety is a recognized exception, the way airlines are allowed to share crash data without it counting as collusion. The interesting detail is that the companies disagree about the legal footing: OpenAI says no permission is required, while Anthropic wants the government to grant it in writing first.

**Why it matters:** If frontier AI rules get settled between the labs rather than in legislation, the companies being regulated are the ones drafting the regulation.

## 2. INTERNATIONAL AFFAIRS

### House votes a third time to end the Iran war, with the largest Republican defection yet

The House passed a war powers resolution 220-204 late Tuesday, September 15, directing President Trump to halt hostilities with Iran without congressional authorization. Seven Republicans joined every Democrat: Reps. Thomas Massie of Kentucky, Brian Fitzpatrick of Pennsylvania, Warren Davidson of Ohio, Tom Barrett of Michigan, Mariannette Miller-Meeks and Zach Nunn of Iowa, and Nancy Mace of South Carolina. It was the biggest Republican crossover of the three votes held since the war began, and none of the three resolutions has reached the president's desk, where a veto would be expected.

**In plain terms:** The Constitution gives Congress the power to declare war, but presidents have run military operations for decades without one. The War Powers Act is Congress's tool for clawing that back: it lets lawmakers formally order a president to stop fighting. The catch is that the order is an ordinary bill, so the president can veto it, and overriding a veto needs two-thirds of both chambers, far above 220 votes. So this changes nothing operationally. What it measures is how many of Trump's own party will now say on the record that the war should end, and that number keeps rising.

**Why it matters:** The vote functions as a defection counter inside the president's party, and the count is climbing seven weeks before midterms in which the war is a live issue.

## 3. ECONOMICS

### The 10-year Treasury yield tops 5% for the first time since 2007 as the Fed meets

The yield on the 10-year US Treasury note climbed above 5% this week, reaching 5.04% before easing to about 5.01%, its highest level since July 2007. Brent crude settled near $109. The Federal Reserve's two-day meeting ends Wednesday, September 16, with a decision at 2 p.m. Eastern, and markets widely expect the first rate increase since 2023, lifting the target range to 3.75%-4.00% from the 3.50%-3.75% held since December 2025. In one market survey, 86% of respondents expected at least one hike and 55% expected more than one. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh warned at Jackson Hole on August 28 that recent inflation data had not shown meaningful improvement in underlying price trends.

**In plain terms:** A Treasury yield is what the US government must pay to borrow money for ten years, and it sets the floor under almost every other interest rate, including mortgages and corporate loans. It rises when investors expect more inflation, or when the government needs to borrow so much that buyers demand a better deal. Both are happening: oil above $100 because of the Iran war, plus heavy federal borrowing. The round number matters less than what it represents, which is safe government bonds now paying enough to compete with stocks. A Fed hike here would reverse three years of direction.

**Why it matters:** Raising rates into a war-driven energy shock means the Fed has decided inflation is the bigger risk than growth, and mortgage and corporate borrowing costs reprice off the 10-year within days.

## 4. HEALTHCARE

### FDA opens applications for a pilot meant to shorten the path to first human drug trials

The FDA began accepting applications on Tuesday, September 15, for its Expedited IND Pilot Program, part of the HHS initiative called Operation TrialBlazer that was announced in June. Sponsors must apply by October 30, and the agency expects to select 8 to 10 sponsor-institution pairs for the first cohort. The program pairs drug developers with vetted "qualified research institutions" to cut the time between the start of IND-enabling work and the first human dose, and to lower the risk of a clinical hold during review. HHS has framed the effort as an attempt to bring early-stage trials back to the US from countries including China and Australia.

**In plain terms:** Before a new drug can be given to a person, the company files an investigational new drug application, or IND, showing the FDA its animal safety data and its trial plan. The FDA can freeze that plan with a "clinical hold," and every round of questions adds months. Many companies now run their first human trials in Australia or China instead, where the process moves faster. This pilot pairs a drug company with an approved research institution before the filing, so problems surface early rather than during review. With only 8 to 10 slots, it is a test of the idea, not yet a change in policy.

**Why it matters:** Where a drug's first human trial runs shapes which country's regulators, hospitals and investors control the rest of its development.

## 5. WILDCARD - AI INDUSTRY

### Chip stocks post their worst day since July after AI leaders call for slowing down

The PHLX semiconductor index fell almost 6% on Monday, September 14, its steepest one-day drop since early July, after Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei published a roughly 3,800-word essay on Saturday, September 12, titled "We Must Pace the Frontier." Nvidia fell 3.4%, AMD 4.4%, Broadcom 4.8%, Micron 5.3% and Intel 5.6%, while SK Hynix dropped 7%. Cybersecurity and software shares moved the other way, with a cybersecurity ETF up about 6%. Amodei asked for one to two additional years of margin before labs reach what he calls critical capability levels. Sam Altman wrote that he agrees "we need to pace the frontier," and Elon Musk replied, "Dario is right."

**In plain terms:** Chipmakers' share prices assume AI companies will keep buying enormous quantities of hardware year after year. When the head of one of those AI companies argues the industry should deliberately go slower, investors hear a warning about future orders. That is why chip and data-center suppliers fell while software and cybersecurity firms rose: those businesses sell to the same customers but do not depend on the hardware buildout continuing. Notice what did not happen, though. No company announced a spending cut. The entire move came from an essay.

**Why it matters:** The AI trade is now sensitive enough that a safety argument alone reprices the semiconductor complex, which makes capital-spending guidance, not model capability, the number to watch.

## 6. WILDCARD - INTERNATIONAL AFFAIRS

### Strait of Hormuz traffic falls to a new low as a tanker strikes mines

Four commodity vessels transited the Strait of Hormuz on Monday, September 14, down from 10 the previous day and against a pre-war norm of roughly 125 large commercial ships a day. On Tuesday the United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations said a vessel had been struck by an unidentified projectile, reporting no damage or environmental impact; Iran's Revolutionary Guard said an oil tanker exploded after hitting naval mines while crossing a prohibited area of the strait. Gulf crude exports are down about 47% from pre-war levels, to roughly 9 million barrels a day from about 17 million in 2025, according to UNCTAD data.

**In plain terms:** The Strait of Hormuz is a channel about 33 kilometres wide at the mouth of the Persian Gulf, and before this war roughly a third of the world's seaborne crude oil passed through it. Iran has mined parts of it and declared sections off-limits, so insurers will not cover most crossings and shipowners will not risk the vessel. That is the mechanism worth understanding: nobody has formally blockaded the strait, and it has closed anyway. It does not take a navy to shut a waterway, only enough uncertainty that captains stop sailing. This is what sits underneath oil at $109 and, indirectly, today's Fed decision.

**Why it matters:** The energy shock driving Western inflation is at bottom an insurance-risk problem, which means it eases when the risk premium falls rather than when a ceasefire is signed.

## 7. WILDCARD - INTERNATIONAL AFFAIRS

### NATO shoots down a drone over Lithuania and Denmark summons Russia's ambassador

NATO fighter jets shot down a drone in Lithuanian airspace overnight into Tuesday, September 15. Lithuanian military officials said it was the first time a drone had been shot down over the country, declined to name its origin pending investigation, and two senior officials said the device was likely carrying explosives. Separately, Denmark said a Russian frigate fired two emergency flares at a Danish air force helicopter conducting routine photography of the ship in the Baltic Sea off the port of Gedser, and summoned Russia's ambassador over the "reckless action." Ambassador Vladimir Barbin blamed "dangerous manoeuvres" by the Danish aircraft.

**In plain terms:** NATO's founding promise is that an attack on one member is an attack on all, which turns every object entering a member's airspace into a question about whether that promise gets triggered. Shooting a drone down is a deliberate decision to treat it as hostile rather than watch and wait, and Lithuania had not done that before. The flare incident is smaller but the same category: two militaries operating close enough that one crew's mistake becomes an international one. Worth noting that Lithuania has not said where the drone came from, so the obvious assumption is not yet established fact.

**Why it matters:** Two NATO-Russia physical contacts in a single day, both resolved at the diplomatic level, show how much of the alliance's eastern-flank stability now depends on individual crews making the right call in seconds.

## 8. WILDCARD - ECONOMICS

### China's retail sales slow again in August as investment keeps contracting

China's retail sales rose 0.4% in August from a year earlier, slowing from 0.6% in July and missing forecasts of 0.8%, according to data released Tuesday, September 15. It was the second consecutive month of slowing growth after a 1% rise in June. Urban fixed-asset investment fell 7.2% in the first eight months of the year, steepening from a 6.7% decline through July. Industrial output went the other way, rising 5.2% and accelerating from 4.5% in July, beating expectations. Beijing's official growth target for the year is 4.5% to 5%, its lowest in decades.

**In plain terms:** China produces far more than its own population buys, so it relies on selling the surplus abroad. These figures show that gap widening rather than closing: factories sped up while household spending came close to flat, and investment in buildings, machinery and infrastructure fell for the eighth straight month. A 7.2% drop in fixed-asset investment means companies and local governments are actively choosing not to build. And 0.4% annual growth in retail sales is barely movement at all once you account for population and prices. Beijing has been trying to fix this since the pandemic ended.

**Why it matters:** A China that makes more and buys less exports deflation and trade friction to everyone else, precisely when Western central banks are raising rates to fight inflation.

## 9. TECH

### SpaceX sets September 22 for Starship's first attempt to reach orbit

SpaceX confirmed that Starship Flight 14 will attempt the vehicle's first orbital flight, deploying a batch of third-generation Starlink satellites on a mission lasting about 10 hours, with reporting on Tuesday, September 15, pointing to a September 22 launch from Starbase, Texas. Every previous Starship flight has been suborbital. It would also be the first Starship launch carrying a revenue-generating payload, though the paying customer is SpaceX's own Starlink division, which operates a constellation of roughly 10,000 satellites. The date has moved more than once: an earlier FCC filing named September 15, and at least one trade outlet reported September 18.

**In plain terms:** "Suborbital" means a rocket goes up and comes back down without ever moving sideways fast enough to keep falling around the Earth instead of into it. Every Starship test so far has done that, which is impressive but is not the same as reaching orbit. Orbit is what lets Starship actually deliver satellites, and delivering satellites is the entire commercial case for building a rocket this large. The satellites aboard are a new, much bigger generation of Starlink that existing rockets cannot carry economically, so the two programs depend on each other. Treat the date as provisional, since it has already slipped twice.

**Why it matters:** Starlink's next-generation capacity is gated on Starship reaching orbit, so this flight sets the clock for how fast satellite broadband capacity grows worldwide.

## 10. TRUMP NEWS

### Trump loses at the Supreme Court on mail ballots and attacks the three justices he appointed

The Supreme Court on Monday declined to let the administration enforce a US Postal Service rule restricting mail-in ballots before the November midterms, leaving in place an injunction issued by US District Judge Indira Talwani. The rule would have required states to adopt a uniform envelope style and submit lists of eligible voters to a Postal Service portal. In a concurrence, Justice Brett Kavanaugh wrote that "there is at least a fair prospect that the final rule falls within the Postal Service's statutory authority," but that "state and local election officials do not have sufficient time to reasonably implement the rule before the elections." Trump posted on Truth Social that it was "a big loss for Republicans, and America itself," and wrote of Justices Kavanaugh, Gorsuch and Barrett, all three of whom he appointed, "These are not the people I interviewed to serve on the United States Supreme Court, they are merely a shell of their original selves." He called Justices Alito and Thomas, who would have granted the request, "legends."

**In plain terms:** The administration wanted the Postal Service to impose new requirements on how states handle mail ballots: one standard envelope design, and a federal portal listing who is eligible to vote. Federal judges froze the rule, and the administration asked the Supreme Court to unfreeze it in time for November. The Court said no. Read Kavanaugh's reasoning carefully, because it is about timing rather than legality: he suggested the rule might well be lawful, but that election officials cannot rewire their systems seven weeks before voting starts. So the rule could still survive later; it just cannot apply this year.

**Why it matters:** Trump put on the public record that he judges his own appointees by whether they rule in his favor, in a term when his administration still has cases pending before the same three justices.
