## 2. INTERNATIONAL AFFAIRS

### World court finds Myanmar responsible for genocide against the Rohingya

The International Court of Justice ruled 9-6 on Thursday that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya in northern Rakhine state. Court President Yuji Iwasawa said Myanmar also failed to prevent and punish genocide and breached the court's 2020 order to preserve evidence. The court ordered investigations and prosecutions, changes to national law, and steps toward refugee return and property restitution, giving Myanmar two years to report on compliance; reparations come in a later phase. The Gambia brought the case in 2019. Myanmar has called it flawed and unfounded.

**In plain terms:** Genocide means trying to destroy a group because of who they are. The Rohingya are a Muslim minority in Myanmar, and more than 700,000 fled to Bangladesh after a military campaign in 2017. This court judges states rather than individuals, and this is the first time it has found a country responsible for genocide. The catch is enforcement: the ruling is binding but the court has no police, so compliance depends on Myanmar's military government or outside pressure.

**Why it matters:** A binding finding of state responsibility hands other governments and courts a settled legal fact to build sanctions, asset claims and prosecutions on, which is where practical consequences will come from.

## 4. HEALTHCARE

### Medicare proposes recovering about $9 billion a year from private insurers

CMS has published its CRUSH proposed rule, CMS-6098-P, which the agency projects will save nearly $90 billion over ten years across Medicare, Medicaid, CHIP and the ACA marketplace. Its largest piece revives the audit rule a Texas court vacated in 2025: CMS would extrapolate findings from a sample of enrollees across an entire Medicare Advantage contract, reach back to payment year 2018 and apply no fee-for-service adjuster, which it estimates collects about $9 billion per payment year. Providers billing Medicare Advantage would also have to enroll in traditional Medicare. Comments are due January 19, 2027. UnitedHealth fell 10.2% Wednesday and about 5.6% Thursday, shedding roughly $50 billion in market value this week.

**In plain terms:** Medicare Advantage is private insurance paid for by the government, and plans are paid more for sicker patients, so there is an incentive to record every diagnosis possible. Auditors check a sample of patient records. Until now, errors found in a sample were charged only for that sample. Extrapolation applies the sample's error rate to the whole contract, turning small findings into very large bills, and reaching back to 2018 reopens eight years of payments. Insurers beat an earlier version of this rule in court in September 2025.

**Why it matters:** The $9 billion is proposed and contested rather than collected, but it puts eight years of Medicare Advantage revenue in play and forces insurers to decide how much exposure to disclose to shareholders.

## 6. WILDCARD - AI INDUSTRY

### House records show Commerce approved 879,868 AI chips for Chinese buyers

The House Foreign Affairs Committee released export-license records on Thursday showing the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security approved 879,868 advanced AI chips for Chinese customers across 21 applications between December 2025 and May 11, 2026, out of about 3.4 million requested. The approvals covered Nvidia H200 and AMD MI325X accelerators; recipients include Tencent at about 150,000 chips, Alibaba at 75,000, Baidu at 43,000 and BYD at 4,000. Committee data indicates more than 30% went to firms on the Pentagon's list of Chinese military companies. Chairman Brian Mast flagged two applicants, Maginfra and New Matrix, as suspected smuggling fronts. Reuters put the total at nearly 900,000.

**In plain terms:** An export license is government permission to sell a controlled product abroad. These chips are the processors used to train large AI systems, and US policy has been to keep the best ones out of China. About one in four requested chips was cleared, which is both a large number and far less than Chinese buyers wanted. Two details sting: a big share went to companies the Pentagon itself lists as tied to China's military, and the committee says two buyers look like smuggling fronts. Appearing on an approved application is not proof a chip was delivered.

**Why it matters:** Congress now holds the paperwork, which turns an argument about export policy into an oversight fight with named companies and exact numbers, and strengthens bills that would give legislators a veto over these licenses.

### California subpoenas Nvidia over whether its chips and secrets reached China

California Attorney General Rob Bonta subpoenaed Nvidia on Thursday, opening an investigation into whether the company's or former chief executive Jensen Huang's actions led to China obtaining advanced chips or confidential information, and whether Nvidia misled investors about that risk. The inquiry follows reporting on Huang's relationship with a woman said to have worked at Megaspeed, a Singapore-based firm under investigation for diverting restricted Nvidia chips to China; Huang resigned on November 12 and CFO Colette Kress is interim chief executive. The Wall Street Journal reported the SEC has opened a separate civil investigation into Nvidia's disclosures. A subpoena is an investigative step, not a finding; no wrongdoing has been established.

**In plain terms:** A subpoena is a legal order to hand over documents, and it starts an investigation rather than proving anything. Two separate questions are being asked. First, did restricted chips or trade secrets reach China, possibly through an intermediary company in Singapore. Second, and this is the part that carries financial penalties, did Nvidia tell its shareholders enough about that risk. Companies are legally required to disclose risks they know about, and a state attorney general can bring civil penalties if they did not.

**Why it matters:** A securities-disclosure case does not require proving a single chip crossed a border, only that Nvidia knew more than it told investors, which is a far lower bar than an export-control prosecution.

## 8. WILDCARD - ECONOMICS

### More Americans are staying on unemployment benefits longer

The Labor Department reported Thursday that initial jobless claims rose to 203,000 for the week ended November 14, above a roughly 197,000 forecast and a second straight week above 200,000. Continuing claims, which count people still drawing benefits, jumped 70,000 to 1.763 million, the highest since late August. Economists read the gap as laid-off workers taking longer to find new jobs rather than a wave of fresh layoffs. October payrolls rose 165,000 with unemployment at 4.1%, and August and September were revised up, to 186,000 and 60,000. The November jobs report is due December 4, four days before the Fed meets.

**In plain terms:** Two different numbers are reported each week. Initial claims count people who just lost a job; continuing claims count people still collecting benefits because they have not found one. Initial claims barely moved, but continuing claims rose sharply, and that combination means hiring is slowing rather than firing speeding up. It matters because it is the harder problem to fix: if companies simply stop opening jobs, lower interest rates would normally be the remedy, and the Fed is expected to raise them in December instead.

**Why it matters:** Rising long-term claims are the first signal that would make a December rate rise politically and economically contentious inside the Fed, and the November jobs report lands four days before the decision.

## 9. TECH

### Chinese hackers used a VMware vCenter flaw for two months before a patch existed

Broadcom released advisory VMSA-2026-0009 on Thursday patching CVE-2026-123890, a critical authentication bypass rated 9.8 in VMware vCenter's directory service that hands an unauthenticated attacker an administrator session. Mandiant attributes exploitation to Armory Castle, a suspected China-nexus espionage group previously tracked as UNC5221, and dates attacks to early September, more than two months before the fix. The intruders entered through a separate BeyondTrust zero-day, CVE-2026-102764, then ran custom tools BONESAW and SAWSNAKE, the latter forging SAML tokens to defeat multi-factor authentication. Fixes ship in vCenter 9.1.1.0200, 9.0.2.0200 and 8.0 U3m; version 7.0 is out of support and unpatched. There is no workaround.

**In plain terms:** vCenter is the control panel companies use to manage every virtual machine in a data centre, so taking it over is closer to holding a building's master key than one office key. A zero-day is a hole attackers find and use before the vendor knows it exists, which is why patching promptly would not have helped here. The detail defenders should note is the multi-factor bypass: one of the tools forged the digital tokens that prove who you are, so the login codes people rely on did not stop it.

**Why it matters:** Two months of undetected administrator access to virtualisation management means the useful question is not whether an organisation has patched but what was taken before the patch existed.

## 10. TRUMP NEWS

### Justice Department drops the Alaska election appeal Trump ordered, and asks the court to erase the ruling

The Justice Department filed in the Ninth Circuit on Thursday to abandon its appeal seeking to delay certification of Alaska's Senate race, writing that "this case has become moot." Trump had directed the department to sue if Republican Senator Dan Sullivan lost, citing voter confusion with a second candidate named Daniel J. Sullivan Jr., a retired teacher. Democrat Mary Peltola won; through preliminary results dated November 18, her margin exceeded the first-choice votes for Sullivan and all other candidates combined. Judge Sharon Gleason dismissed the suit on November 1, two days before the election, finding the government lacked standing and that setting ballots aside would violate the secret ballot; the department appealed the same day. The new filing, from Associate Attorney General Stanley Woodward, also asks the court to vacate Gleason's ruling rather than let it stand. Alaska targets November 25 to certify. A separate FBI inquiry into the second candidate's recruitment continues; no one has been charged.

**In plain terms:** Certification is the step that makes an election result official. The argument was that voters meant to pick Senator Dan Sullivan but chose a different man with nearly the same name, so the count should be paused. A judge threw the case out before anyone voted, holding that the federal government had no standing to sue over a state's ballots. The number that undoes the theory is the margin: Peltola won by more votes than Sullivan and every other candidate received put together. Asking to vacate a ruling means asking to wipe it off the books as precedent.

**Why it matters:** The department spent three weeks appealing a case it has now told the court does not matter, and the request to vacate indicates the remaining concern was the precedent rather than the Alaska result.
