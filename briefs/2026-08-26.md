## 1. AI INDUSTRY

### Nvidia buys the machine that builds AI models, without buying the company

Nvidia is paying Poolside $6 billion for a non-exclusive license to "Model Factory" - the training pipelines, data-curation workflows and reinforcement-learning tooling behind Poolside's open-weight Laguna coding models - and hiring 109 of its engineers. It is separately investing $1 billion at a $12 billion pre-money valuation, roughly quadruple Poolside's prior $3 billion mark. Poolside keeps the software, keeps the right to license it to others, and keeps its three co-founders, among them former GitHub CTO Jason Warner. Reported totals range from $6 billion to $7 billion depending on whether the equity is counted. Tech Times tallied it as Nvidia's third structured non-acquisition in nine months, about $27 billion in all, none of it triggering merger review.

**In plain terms:** Nvidia makes the chips nearly every AI model is trained on. Poolside built the assembly line - the software, data pipelines and accumulated know-how that turn raw text into a working model. Rather than buy Poolside, Nvidia rented the assembly line and hired the 109 people who know how to run it. The distinction is legal, not technical: buying a company triggers an antitrust review, while licensing software and hiring staff usually does not.

**Why it matters:** If a license plus a hiring wave can move a company's core capability without a merger filing, the main instrument regulators have for watching AI consolidation stops seeing most of it.

## 2. INTERNATIONAL AFFAIRS

### Iran and Oman sketch a phased plan to reopen the Strait of Hormuz

Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi and Omani Foreign Minister Badr Albusaidi met in Tehran on Tuesday and described a "phased framework" covering a joint temporary navigational corridor through the Strait of Hormuz and a joint mine-clearing project, with technical talks to continue on a permanent corridor, traffic management and information-sharing. The strait carried about a fifth of the world's seaborne oil and LNG before Iran blockaded it after the US and Israel struck Iran on February 28; Washington answered with a naval counter-blockade and traffic remains largely paralysed. The two accounts differ: Reuters reported the corridor as discussed rather than agreed, with Albusaidi saying he hoped it could be announced soon, while Al Jazeera's Wednesday headline described the two sides as having agreed a new temporary route. Iranian officials continue to say the strait will not fully reopen until the US lifts its blockade, drops oil sanctions and unfreezes Iranian assets.

**In plain terms:** The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow sea passage at the mouth of the Persian Gulf that roughly one in five traded barrels of oil has to squeeze through. Iran closed and mined it in February, tankers stopped, and energy got more expensive everywhere. Oman is the one neighbour both Tehran and Washington still talk to, so it is brokering. The proposal is to clear a single lane and run ships through it under joint supervision, leaving the question of who controls the strait for later. Nothing is signed, and the outlets disagree on whether even the corridor is agreed, so treat reopening as a proposal rather than a fact.

**Why it matters:** One cleared lane would be the first real downward pressure on global energy prices in six months without either side conceding anything about the war itself.

## 3. ECONOMICS

### Canada retaliates with tariffs of up to 50 percent on about $20 billion of US goods

Prime Minister Mark Carney's government announced counter-tariffs of 15 to 50 percent on roughly $20 billion of American imports - steel, dairy, appliances, farm equipment, pulp and paper, electronics, seafood, clothing and cosmetics - taking effect September 8, after Washington imposed 50 percent tariffs on Canadian goods over the weekend when trade talks collapsed. Ottawa paired it with a C$7.5 billion (about US$5.4 billion) support package for affected workers and businesses. Carney said on Saturday, "You're at war when you get attacked. We got attacked," and said an attitude at the table that Canada is "a subsidiary of the United States" is "not something we're going to accept." The US justified its 50 percent rate partly on an allegation that Canada discriminates against American dairy, which Canada has not accepted.

**In plain terms:** A tariff is a tax a government charges on goods arriving from another country. The importer pays it and generally passes it on to shoppers, so tariffs raise prices in the country imposing them, not the country being punished. The US and Canada trade more with each other than almost any two countries on earth. Talks broke down, the US taxed Canadian goods at 50 percent, and Canada is now taxing American ones back, picking products from politically sensitive states. The September 8 start date leaves a two-week window for one side to back down.

**Why it matters:** Both tariff sets hit everyday consumer goods before the US midterms, which turns a negotiating dispute into a visible price story on both sides of the border.

## 4. HEALTHCARE

### FDA clears a new first-line regimen for HER2-positive stomach and esophageal cancer

The FDA on August 25 approved Jazz Pharmaceuticals' Ziihera (zanidatamab-hrii) in two first-line regimens for adults with unresectable or metastatic HER2-positive gastroesophageal adenocarcinoma: with BeiGene's Tevimbra (tislelizumab) plus chemotherapy, and with chemotherapy alone. The decision rests on a 914-patient global Phase 3 trial published in the New England Journal of Medicine in May 2026, in which both zanidatamab arms cut the risk of progression or death by roughly 35 percent and median overall survival reached 26.4 months in the triplet arm against 19.2 months for the control. It is the first bispecific HER2 antibody approved alongside a PD-1 inhibitor and chemotherapy for these patients regardless of PD-L1 status. The approval triggers a $250 million milestone payment from Jazz to Zymeworks, which stays eligible for up to $1.3 billion more plus royalties of up to 20 percent.

**In plain terms:** Gastroesophageal adenocarcinoma is cancer of the stomach and the tube that carries food to it, and it is usually found late. Around one in five of these tumours are driven by a surface protein called HER2 that keeps telling cells to divide. Zanidatamab is an antibody engineered to grab two separate points on HER2 at once, which shuts it down more completely than older drugs that grab one. "First-line" means doctors can now reach for it as the opening treatment instead of after something else has failed, and in the trial patients on the three-drug version lived a median of about seven months longer.

**Why it matters:** A seven-month median survival gain in first-line therapy resets the standard of care in a cancer where the benchmark had barely moved in a decade.

## 5. WILDCARD - INTERNATIONAL AFFAIRS

### US prepares the largest mass visa revocation in its history

The State Department is moving to revoke as many as 200,000 B1 and B2 business and tourism visas issued between 2016 and 2026, targeting people who entered as temporary visitors and later filed for asylum. Spokesman Tommy Pigott said the department is working with the Department of Homeland Security to identify them and that revocations will happen on a rolling basis, so the total will keep changing. Officials described it as the largest mass revocation in US history. Revocation does not by itself cancel a pending asylum application, but it strips the underlying status thousands of people have been relying on while their cases move through the immigration courts.

**In plain terms:** A B1/B2 visa is the ordinary stamp for a business trip or a holiday. Some people arrive on one, decide it is unsafe to go home, and apply for asylum - which is lawful, and the asylum claim is judged on its own merits. The government is now cancelling the original visitor visa for up to 200,000 of those people. Their asylum cases continue, but the document that made them lawfully present is gone, which affects work permits and whether they can be detained. The 200,000 figure is a ceiling officials have named, not a completed action.

**Why it matters:** Removing status while an asylum claim is still pending shifts the practical cost of the wait onto applicants before any judge has ruled on their case.

## 6. WILDCARD - HEALTHCARE

### Study puts a floor of one in four on CTE among former NFL players who died

Researchers from Mass General Brigham, Boston University and the Concussion and CTE Foundation reported in The BMJ on August 25 that at least 25 percent of the 878 former NFL players who died between 2016 and 2021 had chronic traumatic encephalopathy. Of those 878, families of 235 donated brains and 215 of the donated brains showed CTE. Across a wider cohort of 1,712 former players who died between 2008 and 2021, 338 brains were donated and 315 - 93.2 percent - had CTE; nearly 60 percent of donors had dementia, rising to 90.4 percent among those with the most severe stage IV disease. The one-in-four figure is a floor, derived by assuming no CTE at all in the players whose brains were never examined.

**In plain terms:** CTE is brain damage caused by repeated head impacts, and it can still only be confirmed by examining the brain after death. That has always made the numbers arguable, because families who already suspect CTE are the ones most likely to donate - so brain-bank rates near 93 percent clearly overstate the disease across all players. This study did the conservative thing instead. It counted every player who died in the period, assumed every brain that was never donated was healthy, and still arrived at one in four. That is the lowest the true rate can be, not the researchers' best guess.

**Why it matters:** Publishing the result as a floor rather than a sample removes the standard objection to brain-bank studies and hands litigants and regulators a number that survives it.

## 7. WILDCARD - ECONOMICS

### Oil falls to a one-week low after traders read the Iran sanctions as weaker than feared

Brent crude fell more than 3 percent on Tuesday to settle near $87.12 a barrel and West Texas Intermediate near $82.36, a second consecutive decline from Monday's closes of $92.17 and $85.01, after traders concluded the sanctions package Treasury unveiled Monday would not squeeze supply the way the military phase of the conflict had. The measures - "Operation Economic Outcast," announced by Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, designating about sixty entities in Hong Kong, China, Malaysia, the UAE and Singapore and threatening secondary sanctions on anyone still transacting with Iran - set no compliance deadline and left untouched the institutions that clear Iranian oil payments. Saxo Bank's Ole Hansen said the shift from military confrontation to economic coercion had calmed energy markets. Pakistan's army chief spent Tuesday in Tehran, reportedly carrying a sanctions-relief proposal.

**In plain terms:** Sanctions are rules banning companies from doing business with a target. "Secondary" sanctions extend the ban to foreign firms that keep trading anyway, which is the part that usually frightens oil traders. They read the fine print and found two gaps: no date by which anyone has to comply, and no action against the banks that actually move Iran's oil money. Oil is priced on expected future supply, so a threat that removes no barrels pushes prices down rather than up. The market, in other words, marked the announcement as a bluff held in reserve.

**Why it matters:** The price move is the market scoring the policy, and it read the sanctions as written to preserve leverage for later rather than to cut Iran's exports now.

## 8. WILDCARD - INTERNATIONAL AFFAIRS

### Britain hands Ukraine classified missile data so SCALPs can be built there

The UK authorised the defence firm MBDA to release classified information about the British components inside the SCALP cruise missile, clearing the way for Ukraine and France to assemble the weapon on Ukrainian soil. The move accompanied Prime Minister Andy Burnham's visit to Kyiv on Monday for Ukraine's 35th Independence Day, his first foreign trip since taking office, where he co-chaired the Coalition of the Willing and announced the return of ten Ukrainian servicemen previously listed as missing in a prisoner exchange. SCALP is the French version of Britain's Storm Shadow, air-launched with a range above 250 kilometres. Analysts quoted by the Associated Press cautioned that operational missiles are not imminent and do not address Ukraine's most pressing need, air-defence interceptors; Russian drones struck a residential building in Chernihiv on Independence Day, injuring at least 20 people including three children.

**In plain terms:** Storm Shadow and SCALP are the same missile, built by a joint British-French company under two names, and Britain has been donating finished ones since 2023. Donations are capped by how many already exist. Handing over the classified drawings for the British parts lets Ukraine build them at home instead of waiting for deliveries from abroad. The catch is that standing up a missile production line takes years and requires heavy industry that Russia deliberately targets, so this is a decision about 2028 rather than about this winter.

**Why it matters:** Sharing classified component data crosses a line allies had held since 2022, moving from lending Ukraine weapons to giving it the ability to make them.

## 9. TECH

### SpaceX sets late 2027 for its first Nvidia-powered AI satellites

Elon Musk said the first satellites in SpaceX's Starmind constellation, built to run AI workloads in orbit, will launch in the fourth quarter of 2027 and reach "significant scale" in 2028, pulling forward a previously vaguer timeline. SpaceX is designing the compute payload with Nvidia: each Starmind AI1 satellite is to carry roughly 72 Nvidia Rubin GPUs and Vera CPUs, about the contents of one server rack, and to average up to 175 kilowatts. SpaceX has filed with the FCC for a constellation of up to a million such satellites, and anchored its June initial public offering on the argument that orbital data centres will be cheaper and cleaner than ground-based ones.

**In plain terms:** An AI data centre is a warehouse of chips that needs two things in enormous quantity: electricity and cooling. On Earth both are running short, which is why new sites wait years in a queue just to connect to the power grid. In orbit, sunlight never stops and there is no warm air to trap heat, so the pitch is that you skip the queue entirely. The hard parts are that radiation degrades chips, waste heat can only leave by radiating into space rather than by fans, and every kilogram has to be launched. One satellite in 2027 is a demonstration; the business case rests entirely on 2028.

**Why it matters:** If orbital compute works even partly, the binding constraint on AI expansion shifts from grid interconnection queues to launch capacity, which SpaceX already controls.

## 10. TRUMP NEWS

### Judge finds the administration violated her mail-ballot injunction, a day after its Supreme Court win

US District Judge Indira Talwani ruled on August 25 that the administration violated the preliminary injunction she issued in the League of Women Voters of Massachusetts case when the Postal Service issued a final rule on August 21 implementing the president's executive order on mail-ballot design, with Federal Register publication set for August 26. Her injunction barred the administration both from directing USPS to reject non-compliant ballot mail before the 2026 midterms and from "initiating or completing rulemaking" to implement the new criteria. Talwani wrote that "Defendants cannot contend that they misunderstood the scope of the court's order," and concluded that "Defendants violated the preliminary injunction in this case." The government argued in its own August 25 filing that publishing the rule was not a violation because the rule's text says USPS will not implement it while the injunction stands. Talwani found the violation but declined to order any of the remedies the plaintiffs sought, noting her injunction still keeps the rules from taking effect for November. The ruling came a day after the Supreme Court, 6-3, lifted a separate injunction against other parts of the same executive order, with Justice Sonia Sotomayor writing that the decision "does not address whether the President's attempts to interfere with states' administration of the November 2026 elections are lawful."

**In plain terms:** A preliminary injunction is a court order freezing something while a lawsuit runs. This one told the Postal Service two things: do not reject ballots under the new design rules, and do not finish writing those rules. USPS finished writing them anyway, and argued that was permitted because it had promised not to use them yet. The judge said the order plainly covered the writing and not just the using, and that the government could not claim it was confused. She found the violation and imposed no penalty, so the finding sits on the record while the rules stay frozen through the midterms.

**Why it matters:** A violation found without a sanction attached leaves the underlying calculation intact: the rule is written and waiting, and the only thing still holding it is an order this court has now found was disregarded once.
