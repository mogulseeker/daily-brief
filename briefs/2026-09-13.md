## 1. AI INDUSTRY

### Anthropic's chief executive calls for the AI industry to slow down, and OpenAI agrees to his first condition

Dario Amodei published an essay on September 12 titled "We Must Pace the Frontier," arguing frontier labs should deliberately slow capability gains, and committed Anthropic to the first of a three-part plan: giving outside evaluators such as METR permanent, employee-level access to verify safety measures, report incidents and assess how models behave during training. The same day Sam Altman committed OpenAI to matching the pledge, Elon Musk wrote that "Dario is right," and Altman said OpenAI will not list in 2026, pushing its IPO to 2027 at the earliest. The essay followed the September 11 resignation of Anthropic safety researcher Joe Benton, who is joining METR.

**In plain terms:** A "frontier lab" is one of the few companies building the largest AI systems; Anthropic, which makes Claude, is one. Amodei wants outside auditors sitting inside these companies permanently, with employee-level access, rather than testing a finished model from outside. The reason is timing: once training ends, the evidence of how a model behaved during it is largely gone. No lab can slow alone without losing to those that do not, which is why the plan's later steps ask governments to compel it.

**Why it matters:** Two rival labs have now voluntarily surrendered a piece of their own secrecy, which turns "should AI labs be audited" from an abstract policy argument into a concrete standard governments can point at and require of everyone else.

## 2. INTERNATIONAL AFFAIRS

### Saudi Arabia shuts the pipeline that bypasses the Strait of Hormuz as a ship is struck inside the strait

Saudi Arabia shut its 745-mile East-West pipeline, the route that carries its crude to the Red Sea without passing through the Strait of Hormuz, after a drone attack that Riyadh and Baghdad said originated in Iraq. On Sunday morning an Iranian commercial vessel was struck off Qeshm Island inside the strait, killing one of its ten crew, named by Iranian state media as Jamshid Rajabi; the projectile was not identified, the Qeshm governor blamed a "terrorist enemy," and outlets differ on whether three or four others were wounded. Saudi jets struck the Houthi-held Mokha airport on Friday after Houthi forces seized Perim island at the mouth of the Red Sea.

**In plain terms:** Nearly all Gulf oil leaves by sea through the Strait of Hormuz, a narrow channel at the mouth of the Persian Gulf. Saudi Arabia built the East-West pipeline so it would not be trapped if that strait closed, carrying crude overland to the Red Sea instead. Shutting it means the main route and the escape route are compromised at once. The Red Sea exit is contested too, where Houthi forces have taken ground. No one has claimed the strike on the Iranian ship, and Iran's account is the only one so far.

**Why it matters:** Saudi Arabia's ability to keep exporting during a Hormuz shutdown was the assumption holding oil prices below crisis levels, and that assumption is now suspended rather than theoretical.

## 4. HEALTHCARE

### Measles cases hit a post-elimination record while the CDC publicly disputes two state-reported deaths

CDC data through September 10 records 3,294 confirmed US measles cases in 2026, the highest since the country declared measles eliminated in 2000, across 47 jurisdictions, with 95 percent tied to one of 38 outbreaks, 95 percent in people unvaccinated or of unknown status, and 8 percent hospitalized. In August Pennsylvania's governor announced two measles-related deaths; CDC political leadership questioned the state's determinations in a social media post and delayed adding them to the national dashboard. Demetre Daskalakis, who resigned in 2025 as director of the CDC's National Center for Immunization and Respiratory Diseases over political interference, told NPR: "We are seeing a pretty compromised CDC and a director who is at best facilitating non-scientific behavior and at worst colluding with non-scientific behavior."

**In plain terms:** "Elimination" never meant measles vanished; it meant the virus stopped spreading continuously inside the country. Passing 3,294 cases means containment has broken, and the vaccination figure says why: almost everyone infected had no MMR shot or no record of one. The newer problem is counting. Deciding whether a death was caused by measles is the state's job, and the CDC publishes what states find. Here CDC leadership publicly disputed a state's finding and withheld the numbers, so the national dashboard hospitals rely on may no longer be neutral.

**Why it matters:** Once the federal case and death counts are seen as negotiable, every downstream decision that depends on them, from school policies to hospital surge planning, loses its shared reference point just as flu season starts.

## 5. WILDCARD - AI INDUSTRY

### Hundreds of AI agents ran an automated hacking campaign against 395 organizations

Security firm GreyNoise reported that from August 31 a likely Russian-speaking actor used hundreds of AI agents, built on OpenAI's Codex harness and a DeepSeek model alongside public offensive-security tools, to chain two PaperCut NG/MF flaws: CVE-2026-81578, an authentication bypass, and CVE-2026-82078, remote code execution. At least 440 servers at 395 organizations across 48 countries were compromised, with credentials taken from 280 victims, operating-system or domain secrets from 147, and administrator privileges at 12; about half the victims were schools and universities. First remote code execution came under four hours in, and at peak the agents compromised 11 organizations in 26 seconds.

**In plain terms:** PaperCut manages office and campus printing, which sounds trivial, but it sits inside the network with permission to query user directories, so breaking it opens doors elsewhere. The two flaws work as a pair: one gets in without a password, the other runs the attacker's code. What is new is not the flaws but the labor. Doing this target by target is slow human work, and that slowness is what normally gives defenders time to patch. Hundreds of agents in parallel erase that window.

**Why it matters:** The bottleneck in mass exploitation has always been attacker time rather than attacker knowledge, and this campaign is the first well-documented case of that bottleneck being removed at scale.

**Why it matters:** A defense lending program aimed at supply chains is now underwriting commercial AI infrastructure, which folds data-center capacity into the same national-security category as rare earths and drones.

## 8. WILDCARD - HEALTHCARE

### A flu vaccine combining three separate boosting methods for older adults published its phase 3 results

CSL Seqirus announced on September 11 that pivotal phase 3 results for its MF59-adjuvanted, cell-based, higher-dose quadrivalent influenza vaccine were published in The Lancet Infectious Diseases, from a randomized controlled trial of immunogenicity and safety in adults aged 50 and older. It is the first flu vaccine to combine three established ways of strengthening protection in older people in one product: an adjuvant, cell-based rather than egg-based manufacturing, and a larger antigen dose. The European Commission has approved it as Aujemflu for adults 50 and over; detailed efficacy figures come from the company's announcement rather than independent reporting, and this brief has not verified them against the paper.

**In plain terms:** Flu vaccines work poorly in older people because immune systems weaken with age, which is why most flu deaths are in people over 65. Makers have three separate fixes. An adjuvant is an additive that provokes a stronger immune response. Cell-based manufacturing grows the vaccine virus in cultured cells instead of chicken eggs, avoiding the small mutations viruses pick up adapting to eggs. A higher dose simply gives more of the target protein. This stacks all three. Immunogenicity trials measure antibodies in blood, not how many people avoid getting sick.

**Why it matters:** Flu vaccine effectiveness in people over 65 has been the stubborn gap in seasonal vaccination for decades, and combining the three known fixes is the first serious test of whether they add up or overlap.

## 9. TECH

### The FBI is investigating a leak of 153 million driver's license scans as lawsuits pile up

Identity-verification company IDScan.net confirmed that data in its cloud platform was accessed without authorization, after journalist Brian Krebs reported on September 1 that a dark-web service called Nexus was selling more than 153 million US and Canadian driver's license scans, plus 10 million ID cards, 3 million travel documents and 579,000 medical cards. IDScan disclosed the incident on September 4, the FBI's New Orleans field office confirmed last week that it is investigating, and at least four class actions have been filed in the US District Court for the Eastern District of Louisiana, with some trackers counting eight. IDScan processes more than 21 million ID checks a month for customers including Hertz, Target, FedEx and Caesars Entertainment.

**In plain terms:** When a bar, rental counter or dispensary scans your license, the business usually does not check it itself; it sends the scan to a verification vendor, which keeps the image. IDScan is one of those vendors, so one breach exposes everyone scanned by any of its customers, most of whom have never heard of it. What leaked is not a password you can change but a photograph of a government ID. The 153 million figure is the seller's claim, checked in part by Krebs against samples, not confirmed by IDScan.

**Why it matters:** The identity-verification layer built to stop fraud has become a single point of failure that concentrates the documents fraud is committed with, and no consumer chose to be in it.

## 10. TRUMP NEWS

### A judge blocked the requirement that civil-service applicants write about Trump's executive orders

US District Judge George A. O'Toole Jr. entered a preliminary injunction in Boston on Friday, September 11, barring the Office of Personnel Management from placing on federal job listings a question directing applicants to "identify one or two relevant Executive Orders or policy initiatives that are significant to [the applicant], and explain how [the applicant] would help implement them if hired." More than 70,000 postings for nonpartisan career positions carried it. O'Toole wrote that "the plaintiffs are thus likely to succeed in showing that there is no legitimate governmental interest in inquiring into the political beliefs of applicants to federal civil service vacancies," finding the question likely violated the Administrative Procedure Act and the First Amendment; the plaintiffs were the American Federation of Government Employees, AFSCME and the National Association of Government Employees. This is a preliminary ruling, and O'Toole denied the unions' broader request to bar the government from weighing answers already submitted.

**In plain terms:** The federal civil service is deliberately walled off from politics: about two million career jobs are filled on merit, while only a few thousand appointees change with each president. That rule dates to 1883, after a system of handing jobs to the winning party's supporters left the government unable to function between elections. OPM runs federal hiring, and it added an essay asking applicants which of the president's executive orders mattered to them. The injunction stops the question going forward; it does not undo hiring already done with the answers.

**Why it matters:** The order establishes, at least provisionally, that the administration could not articulate any lawful purpose for the question, which is a harder finding to work around than a procedural defect it could cure and re-issue.
