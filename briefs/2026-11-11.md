## 1. AI INDUSTRY

### OpenAI's quarterly loss widens to $10.9 billion as revenue reaches $10 billion

OpenAI shared figures with investors on November 10 showing third-quarter revenue of about $10 billion, up roughly 50% from the previous quarter, while its adjusted operating loss widened to $10.9 billion from $10.1 billion, according to financial documents reviewed by The Wall Street Journal. The adjusted figure excludes $2.6 billion of stock-based compensation, so the full gap is wider, and loss as a share of revenue improved to 109% from 150%. CFO Sarah Friar has placed the annual revenue run rate at roughly $50 billion.

**In plain terms:** OpenAI is the company behind ChatGPT. An operating loss is the gap between what a business earns and what it costs to run, so this one spent about two dollars for every dollar it took in. Training and running AI models requires enormous computing power, and that bill arrives whether customers show up or not. The direction matters more than the number: the gap is growing in dollars while shrinking as a share of revenue.

**Why it matters:** OpenAI now loses more in a quarter than most public companies earn in a year, which makes continued access to capital rather than model quality the binding constraint on what it can build next.

## 2. INTERNATIONAL AFFAIRS

### Russia and Ukraine agree to a prisoner exchange as US-mediated talks end without a ceasefire

Two days of US-mediated talks in Abu Dhabi closed on Wednesday with an agreement in principle to a large prisoner-of-war exchange and no movement on a ceasefire or territory, ending nine months without direct Russia-Ukraine negotiations. US envoy Jared Kushner called it the biggest swap of the war but said final numbers would be set Thursday; Russian delegation head Vladimir Medinsky and Ukraine's Rustem Umerov both confirmed the plan. Kushner and Steve Witkoff fly to Moscow on Thursday to brief Vladimir Putin, then to Ireland to meet Volodymyr Zelensky.

**In plain terms:** A prisoner-of-war exchange is a trade of captured soldiers, usually in matched numbers. It is the easiest thing two warring governments can agree to, because neither gives up anything it is fighting for. That is precisely why it happened and a ceasefire did not: the hard questions, who stops shooting first and who keeps which land, went untouched. No final count exists yet, so "biggest of the war" is a claim rather than a confirmed figure.

**Why it matters:** Nine months of silence ended with the one agreement that required no concession from either side, which is a measure of how far apart the two governments remain on everything that would actually stop the fighting.

## 3. ECONOMICS

### October inflation came in hot, pushing the Fed toward a December rate increase

Consumer prices rose 0.4% in October against the 0.3% economists expected, and core prices excluding food and energy rose 0.3% for a third consecutive month, the Labor Department reported Tuesday, with shelter accounting for nearly a third of the increase. No official year-over-year rate was published, because the 2025 government shutdown left a hole in the data series; economists estimate roughly 4%, up from 3.7% in September. Market pricing put the odds of a quarter-point increase at the December 9 meeting near 85%, from the current 3.75%-4.00% range.

**In plain terms:** The Federal Reserve raises interest rates to slow inflation: borrowing gets more expensive, so spending cools. Core inflation strips out food and fuel because those swing wildly month to month, which leaves the underlying trend visible. Three straight months at 0.3% is the part that worries the Fed, because it suggests the pace has settled in rather than spiked once. And there is no official annual inflation figure at all this month, because last year's shutdown broke the data series.

**Why it matters:** The Fed is preparing to tighten using an inflation series that is missing its own headline annual number, so policy is being set with less information than usual at the moment the direction is changing.

## 4. HEALTHCARE

### FDA orders an eye-damage warning onto every GLP-1 diabetes and weight-loss drug

The FDA said on November 9 that it is requiring every maker of a GLP-1 receptor agonist to add a warning about non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy, a rare optic-nerve injury that can cause sudden and permanent vision loss, to the Warnings and Precautions section of the label. The requirement covers semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy), tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound), the newly approved oral orforglipron and older drugs including exenatide and liraglutide. The agency's pooled analysis found 6.4 NAION cases per 100,000 person-years on GLP-1 drugs against 3.8 per 100,000 on placebo or comparators, a difference that was statistically significant against DPP-4 inhibitors (hazard ratio 1.43) but not against SGLT-2 inhibitors.

**In plain terms:** GLP-1 drugs are the injections and pills now taken by millions for diabetes and weight loss. NAION is effectively a stroke in the optic nerve, the cable from eye to brain: blood flow stops and the lost vision does not return. The FDA's own numbers show a small risk, about six cases per 100,000 people a year against four without. But diabetes, obesity and sleep apnea are themselves NAION risk factors, so separating drug from disease is hard. The FDA says "potential increased risk," not proof of cause.

**Why it matters:** A class-wide label change on drugs taken by millions makes this warning a standard part of the prescribing conversation, which is how a rare risk starts changing real prescribing behavior.

## 5. WILDCARD - INTERNATIONAL AFFAIRS

### US pulls refueling tankers out of a British air base over an Iran threat

The US Air Force moved KC-135 Stratotanker refueling aircraft out of RAF Mildenhall in England on Monday, with reporting putting the number at nine or ten and flight tracking showing arrivals in Germany, Spain and Scotland. The Wall Street Journal first tied the move to an Iranian threat, which has not been described publicly, and both Mildenhall and nearby RAF Lakenheath were raised to force protection condition Charlie, the second-highest US defensive posture. The military said that "due to operations security, we will not provide further information on movement of assets," calling such relocations routine; Britain said its bases remain safe.

**In plain terms:** Air-refueling tankers are flying fuel stations; without them, fighters and bombers cannot reach distant targets and return. They are large, slow and parked in the open, among the most valuable and easiest things on a base to hit. Moving them, and raising the threat level at two nearby bases, says the US judged the risk to parked aircraft higher than the cost of scattering them. The count comes from flight trackers, not any official statement, and the threat itself is undisclosed.

**Why it matters:** Dispersing tankers is what a military does when it expects to be shot at rather than when it expects to strike, which makes this a read on how exposed US commanders now think their own bases are.

## 6. WILDCARD - ECONOMICS

### ECB officials line up behind a December rate increase

ECB chief economist Philip Lane told Bloomberg Television it "would be prudent indeed to hike" in December if incoming data match what the bank already sees, and Bundesbank President Joachim Nagel said Tuesday there is a "strong case" for an increase, with Latvia's Martins Kazaks backing "some further tightening." Markets expect a quarter-point rise to a 2.75% deposit rate at the December 17 meeting, and Reuters has reported that policymakers expect to pause after it because higher bond yields are already restraining growth and inflation.

**In plain terms:** The European Central Bank sets interest rates for the twenty countries that use the euro. The deposit rate is what banks earn for parking money there overnight, and raising it pulls borrowing costs up across the economy. The signal is the chorus rather than any single remark: three senior officials said the same thing within two days, which is how the ECB telegraphs a decision before taking it. The planned pause afterward matters too, because rising bond yields are already tightening.

**Why it matters:** Europe and the United States are now set to raise rates in the same month, so the two largest advanced economies would be tightening together rather than one offsetting the other through the exchange rate.

## 7. WILDCARD - HEALTHCARE

### Beam's gene-edited sickle cell therapy meets its goal in a pivotal trial

Beam Therapeutics said Tuesday that risto-cel met the primary endpoint of its pivotal BEACON Phase 1/2 trial, freedom from severe vaso-occlusive pain crises for at least 12 consecutive months, at a prespecified interim analysis of 31 patients who completed 12-month follow-up out of 49 treated as of an October 20 cutoff. All primary safety endpoints were met, but Beam disclosed neither how many patients achieved the endpoint nor any p-value. It expects to file a Biologics License Application as early as the end of 2026, which would make risto-cel the first base-editing therapy submitted for FDA approval.

**In plain terms:** Sickle cell disease warps red blood cells into a crescent shape that jams in small blood vessels, causing bouts of severe pain called vaso-occlusive crises. Base editing rewrites a single letter of DNA in a patient's own blood stem cells instead of cutting the strand, as older CRISPR approaches do. The treatment is harsh: chemotherapy clears the existing bone marrow before the edited cells go back in, so safety matters as much as efficacy. Beam withheld the actual response numbers.

**Why it matters:** A single-letter DNA edit reaching an FDA filing turns base editing from a laboratory technique into a regulated medical product, and that review will set the evidence bar for everything that follows it.

## 8. WILDCARD - INTERNATIONAL AFFAIRS

### COP31 opens in Turkey with the leaders of the three biggest emitters absent

Around 40 heads of state and government, among them Brazil's Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva, Turkey's Recep Tayyip Erdogan and Australia's Anthony Albanese, addressed the UN climate summit in Antalya on Wednesday, while the leaders of the United States, China and India did not attend. UN Secretary-General Antonio Guterres told delegates the climate crisis is "outrunning us." The talks run to November 20 under an unusual co-presidency agreed at COP30, with Turkish environment minister Murat Kurum as president and Australia's Chris Bowen running negotiations toward a plan to raise electricity's share of global energy use and a revival of stalled language on moving away from fossil fuels.

**In plain terms:** COP is the annual UN meeting where countries negotiate climate commitments, and because decisions require consensus, any one country can block the text. Raising electricity's share of energy use is the technical core of cutting emissions, because electricity can come from sources that burn nothing while a furnace or a car engine cannot. The absence of the US, China and India leaders is the story: they account for the largest share of global emissions, and a consensus process without them binds nobody.

**Why it matters:** A summit whose stated goal is reviving fossil-fuel transition language is attempting it without the leaders of the countries that would have to do most of the transitioning.

## 9. TECH

### Japan's state-backed chipmaker Rapidus is likely to miss its 2-nanometer deadline

Rapidus has not yet produced a working SRAM cell, a milestone it needed to clear by summer 2026 to begin 2-nanometer mass production by March 2028, Bloomberg reported Tuesday citing people familiar with the matter. Jiji Press reported the company is weighing a one-year slip to fiscal 2028, which runs from April 2028 to March 2029, because of difficulties in technology development, while Rapidus said it will continue to do everything possible to start mass production in the latter half of fiscal 2027. Japan's government has committed more than 3 trillion yen in subsidies and equity through fiscal 2027.

**In plain terms:** A label like "2nm" is a marketing name for a generation of chipmaking, not a physical measurement, and only TSMC, Samsung and Intel can do it today. Rapidus is Japan's state-funded attempt to join them from a standing start, using technology licensed from IBM. An SRAM cell is the fast memory block built into a chip and the standard first test of whether a process works at all. Failing to produce a working one puts the problem at the foundation, not final assembly.

**Why it matters:** Japan has put more than 3 trillion yen behind the premise that leading-edge chipmaking can be rebuilt domestically within a decade, and the first hard test of that premise is slipping.

## 10. TRUMP NEWS

### Ordered to fly back a man it deported to Central African Republic, DHS argued the flight cost too much

Judge Jeffrey Bryan of the US District Court in Minnesota has denied the Department of Homeland Security's request to pause his order returning Kenis Lopez Gonzalez, a 42-year-old Nicaraguan asylum seeker deported to Central African Republic, a country he has no ties to; among the reasons DHS gave was that flying him back during its appeal would cost too much money. Bryan wrote that the cost "comprises an insignificant fraction (possibly as small as one-millionth) of the billions that DHS is allocated each year," and that the danger Lopez Gonzalez faces outweighs those travel expenses. In his October 30 order Bryan found the government had likely violated federal regulations and constitutional due process in detaining and deporting him; DHS has accused Bryan of "openly defying the Supreme Court" and is appealing to the Eighth Circuit.

**In plain terms:** An asylum seeker is someone asking for protection because they say they face danger at home. Lopez Gonzalez was living in Minnesota, was detained in January and was deported not to Nicaragua but to Central African Republic, where he has no family, language or legal status. A judge found that probably unlawful and ordered him brought back; the government asked to wait, and one reason it gave in writing was the price of the flight. "Likely" violated is the standard at this stage.

**Why it matters:** The cost objection came from the government's own filing, which puts the expense of returning one wrongly deported man on the record as a reason the administration offered a federal court for not doing it.
