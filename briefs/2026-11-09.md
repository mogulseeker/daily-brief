## 1. AI INDUSTRY

### Nvidia's board hires outside counsel to investigate its own chief executive

Nvidia's board said Sunday that a committee of independent directors, with outside counsel, will investigate chief executive Jensen Huang's relationship with Alice Huang, a businesswoman of no relation whom US intelligence officials believe passed information to Beijing, as The New York Times and Financial Times reported. Huang acknowledged the affair in a staff email and wrote, "I am deeply sorry." She is affiliated with Megaspeed, a cloud company the Times reported bought about $4.8 billion of Nvidia equipment. The board said it has no reason to believe secrets or national security were compromised, and Huang keeps his job. Shares fell about 5% in premarket trading.

**In plain terms:** Nvidia designs the chips almost every AI system is trained on, which makes it both the world's most valuable company and a standing target for intelligence services. Its board, the directors who oversee a chief executive on behalf of shareholders, has hired outside lawyers to examine its own boss. The worry is not the affair but the business beside it: the woman is tied to a company that bought roughly $4.8 billion of Nvidia hardware.

**Why it matters:** A board that keeps a chief executive while hiring outside counsel to investigate him has handed the verdict to whoever reads the report first, and Congress is in line ahead of shareholders.

## 2. INTERNATIONAL AFFAIRS

### Israel's president passes over Netanyahu and hands the mandate to Gadi Eisenkot

President Isaac Herzog on Sunday gave Gadi Eisenkot, leader of the Yashar party and a former army chief of staff, 28 days to form a government, with a possible 14-day extension. Herzog said factions holding 67 seats in the newly elected 26th Knesset recommended Eisenkot against 46 for caretaker Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, whose Likud remains the largest single party after the October 27 election. Eisenkot said Israel is in a state of emergency and pledged a national emergency government. He has ruled out a coalition with Arab parties, whose members recommended him anyway.

**In plain terms:** Israelis vote for parties, not for a prime minister. The president then asks whichever leader has the most declared support to assemble a majority of the 120-seat Knesset. Netanyahu's party won the most seats, but more members said they would serve under Eisenkot, so he gets the first attempt. The catch is arithmetic: the 67 backing him include Arab parties he refuses to sit with, so his own red line erases his majority.

**Why it matters:** Netanyahu has been the default answer to this question since 2009, and for the first time in seventeen years the arithmetic is someone else's problem to solve.

## 3. ECONOMICS

### Citigroup pulls money out of US stocks and into Europe after the post-midterm AI selloff

Citigroup strategists led by Beata Manthey cut US equities and global technology to neutral from overweight on Monday and raised European stocks excluding the UK to overweight, citing AI turbulence after the Democratic sweep of the November 3 midterms and the benefit Europe draws from cheaper oil. They also moved healthcare to overweight and warned Nasdaq positioning could slip into net-short. Goldman Sachs noted Europe outperformed the US in all eight of the largest AI-led selloffs since 2023. October CPI is due Tuesday; futures put December Fed hike odds near 80%, the target range at 3.75% to 4.00%.

**In plain terms:** Large banks publish a house view telling clients how much to hold in each region and industry. Overweight means own more than the benchmark; neutral means own about the same. Citi has moved America and technology from own-more to own-about, and Europe to own-more. The reasoning is not that Europe's economy is stronger but that it owns less of the AI trade, so it falls less when AI stocks fall.

**Why it matters:** When the sell side starts recommending Europe over America on crowding rather than growth, the AI trade has stopped being a conviction and become a position everyone is trying to leave through the same door.

## 4. HEALTHCARE

### Novartis kills its lipoprotein(a) drug after it cut the target by 78% and prevented nothing

Novartis said it will discontinue pelacarsen in atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease after the Lp(a)HORIZON trial, presented Saturday by the Cleveland Clinic's Steven Nissen at the American Heart Association meeting and published in the New England Journal of Medicine. In about 8,300 patients with prior cardiovascular events, the drug cut lipoprotein(a) by roughly 78% at 12 months, but major cardiovascular events occurred in 12.3% on pelacarsen against 12.1% on placebo over a median 4.7 years, a hazard ratio of 1.02. Discontinuations for adverse events ran 20.6% versus 7.5%, with 19 anaphylaxis cases on drug and none on placebo.

**In plain terms:** Lipoprotein(a) is a blood particle, set almost entirely by the genes you are born with, that raises heart-attack risk and that diet, exercise and statins barely touch. Because people who inherit low levels have fewer heart attacks, drugmakers assumed lowering it in adults would work the same way. Novartis lowered it by nearly four-fifths and the heart attacks came at the same rate. Nissen's explanation is that these patients were already too far along.

**Why it matters:** Three more companies are running the same experiment with different molecules, and each now has to argue the biology is sound but Novartis chose the wrong patients.

### American, Russian and Ukrainian delegations meet in Abu Dhabi on Tuesday, the first trilateral talks since February

Working-level negotiations open Tuesday and run through Wednesday in Abu Dhabi, mediated by the United States, with Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff leading the American delegation and Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov telling Interfax that Russia's team will include senior military officials. National security officials from the EU, NATO, France, Germany and the UK will be on site, with their role undefined. The agenda covers an energy and maritime ceasefire, prisoner exchanges and the return of Ukrainian children taken to Russia. Ukraine wants an immediate ceasefire first; Russia wants a comprehensive settlement. Rounds in January and February broke off when the Iran war began.

**In plain terms:** Working-level means the officials who draft text, not the leaders who sign it, so no agreement is expected this week. The two sides disagree about sequence as much as substance: Ukraine wants the shooting stopped and the hard questions settled later, while Russia wants them settled first and keeps fighting as leverage. Hence the narrow agenda: ships, prisoners and abducted children are the few items available without conceding the war.

**Why it matters:** The narrowness of the agenda is the information: both capitals are testing whether the other will honour anything at all before either will discuss a ceasefire.

## 6. WILDCARD - AI INDUSTRY

### Two Chinese firms cleared by Commerce to handle Nvidia's H200 chips have empty offices

A New York Times investigation published Saturday reported that New Matrix, a Beijing company incorporated 17 months ago, lists a registered address reporters found to be a vacant industrial unit, and that Maginfra's office was deserted when they visited in September. Both are among roughly two dozen Chinese entities the Commerce Department has licensed in connection with H200 sales, and neither was cleared as an end user, only to assemble the chips into AI servers for unnamed clients. The Times reported a New Matrix shareholder is linked to Wise Road Capital, added to the US Entity List in 2024, and that Maginfra shares staff and patents with the blacklisted server maker Inspur. The Bureau of Industry and Security said it reviews every application carefully.

**In plain terms:** An export licence is the US government's written judgment that a named foreign buyer can be trusted with a controlled American product. Reporters tested that judgment the simplest way available, by visiting the registered addresses, and found units nobody occupies. Neither company was cleared to use the chips itself, only to bolt them into servers and pass them on. So the licence follows the box, never the customer.

**Why it matters:** Export controls are worth exactly as much as the vetting behind them, and a newly Democratic Congress now has a documented vetting failure it can subpoena.

## 7. WILDCARD - AI INDUSTRY

### Anthropic bought 1.7 gigawatts of gas generators for about $3.4 billion to power its own data centers

The Financial Times reported that Anthropic agreed in late September to pay roughly $3.4 billion for 1.7 gigawatts of gas-fired generating capacity, taking over Caterpillar generator orders placed by OnePWR Solutions, a San Antonio supplier. The FT called it the largest known single purchase of power equipment by an AI company, and said no OnePWR staff moved across and no site was named. A Piper Sandler disclosure instead describes a $3.4 billion sale of OnePWR's power-generation platform to an unnamed AI lab, so the structure is unsettled though both agree on the price. Anthropic disclosed on November 2 that it intends to spend more than $895 billion, mostly on compute.

**In plain terms:** A data center cannot run on a contract with a utility; it needs electricity physically delivered, and the queue to connect a big new load to the US grid now runs for years. Buying generators is how you skip the queue, making your own power on site by burning gas. 1.7 gigawatts is roughly a large nuclear reactor's output. The two accounts of the deal differ, so treat its structure as unconfirmed.

**Why it matters:** An AI company buying its own power plants prices the real bottleneck in the buildout, and it is electricity rather than chips.

## 8. WILDCARD - INTERNATIONAL AFFAIRS

### COP31 opens in Antalya with an immediate fight over money and no US government delegation

The UN climate conference opened Monday in Antalya and runs through November 20, with Turkiye holding the presidency under Environment Minister Murat Kurum and Australia's Chris Bowen leading negotiations. Turkish officials expected close to 100 heads of state and government and about 300 ministers, with a leaders' summit on November 11 and 12. UN Secretary-General Antonio Guterres told delegates to "get serious" after a year of weather extremes. The opening ceremony began 90 minutes late without explanation, and the first dispute was over climate finance. President Trump sent no government officials.

**In plain terms:** A COP is the annual meeting where roughly 190 governments try to agree the next step on emissions, and nearly every dispute becomes a dispute about who pays. Poorer countries, which did least to cause the warming, want firm money for adapting to it; richer ones want the obligation shared and the wording loose. The absence that matters is the United States, the largest historical emitter, which sent nobody.

**Why it matters:** With Washington out of the room, the question stops being what the US will accept and becomes whether anyone else will fund what it declines to.

## 9. TECHNOLOGY

### Solana developers move to delete the transaction feature used to drain $270 million from Drift

A proposal filed November 5 by Anza contributor Gabe Rodriguez, SIMD-0723, would retire Solana's durable nonces no earlier than May 5, 2027. Attackers used the feature on April 1 to take at least $270 million from the Drift exchange, firing off transactions Drift's security council had signed more than a week earlier; some loss estimates run to about $285 million. Ordinary Solana transactions expire in roughly 30 seconds, while a durable nonce keeps a signed transaction valid indefinitely. The proposal still needs technical approval, and custodians that sign on offline machines would have to rebuild how they move funds.

**In plain terms:** A blockchain transaction is signed with a secret key and then broadcast. Solana normally gives a signed transaction about half a minute to land, which limits the damage if a signature leaks. Durable nonces removed that clock so institutions could sign on a computer kept permanently off the internet, safer because the key never touches a network. The Drift attackers used the other half of that trade, firing week-old signatures.

**Why it matters:** The fix is a reminder that the large crypto losses come from how custody is operated rather than from broken cryptography.

## 10. TRUMP NEWS

### Trump calls his own deportation policy "too tough" hours before promoting the man who built it

In a 60 Minutes interview taped Friday and aired Sunday, Trump told Ross Douthat of his immigration crackdown, "I think it was too tough," adding, "And I think -- I think ultimately, that hurt me." He cited immigrants in the country ten years or more without serious crimes, while claiming an A+ on the border. He campaigned in 2024 on "the largest deportation operation in American history"; ICE book-ins hit a second-term high above 46,000 in July 2026, and PolitiFact's tracker records removals far short of the million-a-year goal. The interview was taped hours before the White House said Stephen Miller, the policy's architect, would replace Susie Wiles as chief of staff. Trump separately blamed Republican midterm losses on the Iran war, gasoline prices and "a great dislike -- incredibly -- for Israel."

**In plain terms:** Trump ran promising to deport more people than any president in American history, built the machinery to attempt it, and has now told an interviewer it went too far and cost him votes. He did not say he would change it. On the day the interview was taped, the official who designed the policy was promoted to the most powerful staff job in the White House.

**Why it matters:** An admission that comes with no policy change and a promotion for the architect is a statement about messaging rather than about enforcement, and the personnel decision made the same day says which one he meant.
