## 1. AI INDUSTRY

### Meta seeks $97.5 billion of equity for AI infrastructure, the largest such raise on record

Meta priced $25 billion of common stock at $625 a share, upsized from $22.5 billion, plus $12.5 billion of mandatory convertible preferred at $50 per depositary share, and disclosed an at-the-market programme of up to $60 billion that begins once its new non-voting Class C shares start trading, expected in the first quarter of 2027. The roughly $97.5 billion total would be the largest equity raise on record. Morgan Stanley and Goldman Sachs are leading the offerings. Nvidia said it will buy $3 billion of stock, and Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom together signalled interest of up to $5 billion in Class A shares.

**In plain terms:** Meta owns Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp. Building AI means buying enormous numbers of specialised chips and housing them in buildings wired for huge amounts of electricity, which costs more cash than Meta's business generates. So instead of paying out of profits it is selling new shares, which are ownership slices of the company. An at-the-market programme drips those shares onto the stock exchange over months rather than all at once. Note that Nvidia, which sells Meta the chips, is buying $3 billion of the shares.

**Why it matters:** Funding for the AI build-out has shifted from operating cash flow to capital markets, which means outside investors rather than earnings now set the pace.

### Brazilian assets sell off after Lula's re-election and the Treasury pulls two bond auctions

The Ibovespa triggered B3's circuit breaker at a 10 percent fall, halting trading for 30 minutes, and closed Monday down 13.88 percent at 176,608, its steepest one-day drop since March 2020; at the intraday low near 169,516 it was off about 17 percent. The dollar gained 8.79 percent against the real to 5.4154, from roughly 4.99 before the vote. Brazil's Treasury cancelled bond auctions scheduled for October 27 and October 29. Lula beat Flavio Bolsonaro by about 4.4 million votes after trailing in the first round; economists quoted by Al Jazeera attributed the scale of the move to traders unwinding positions that had assumed a Bolsonaro win.

**In plain terms:** The Ibovespa is Brazil's main stock index, as the S&P 500 is for the United States. A circuit breaker is an automatic pause in trading when prices fall too fast, meant to stop a panic. Investors had bet heavily that Bolsonaro would win and cut spending; when Lula won they had to sell those bets at once, which is why a single day's fall was so large. The Treasury pulled its debt auctions because selling bonds on such a day means paying much higher interest.

**Why it matters:** The cancelled auctions are the concrete cost: Brazil has to keep refinancing its debt, and every week it waits for calmer markets risks a higher price for it.

## 4. HEALTHCARE

### Bon Secours affiliate will take Surgery Partners private in a $5.7 billion deal

HealthSpan Partners, an affiliate of the Catholic nonprofit Bon Secours Mercy Health, will buy Surgery Partners for $23.50 a share in cash, about a 49 percent premium to the prior close, valuing the company near $5.7 billion including debt. Bain Capital affiliates, which hold about 39 percent of the shares, will roll their stake into the acquiring entity rather than cash out. A special committee of independent directors negotiated the deal, which is expected to close in the first quarter of 2027. It adds more than 200 outpatient surgical sites across 30 states and about 5,000 affiliated physicians to a system with more than 45 hospitals, and follows Ascension's $3.9 billion purchase of AmSurg.

**In plain terms:** An ambulatory surgery centre is a small facility where you have an operation and go home the same day, such as a cataract removal, and it costs far less to run than a hospital operating room. Surgery Partners owns hundreds of them. A large hospital charity is buying the whole company and taking it off the stock market. Bain Capital, the investment firm that owns about two-fifths of it, is keeping its stake rather than selling, which signals it expects more value under the new owner.

**Why it matters:** Hospital systems are buying the cheaper outpatient settings that had been taking surgical volume away from them, which concentrates who sets the price of a routine operation.

### Brussels moves to let Frontex carry out deportations between countries outside the EU

Euronews, citing EU officials, reported that the European Commission will propose letting Frontex, the bloc's border agency, transfer migrants from one non-EU country to another and take part in removals from return hubs outside the Union, for instance helping North African authorities return Central African nationals before they reach Europe. A 2025 Council note acknowledged that the current Frontex regulation does not explicitly permit assistance with returns between third countries. Euronews put publication on Tuesday, October 27; other reporting points to October 28. In July the Commission's Olivier Onidi said any such operation would need a "fully-fledged international agreement" backed by member states and Parliament, both of which must also approve the proposal itself.

**In plain terms:** Frontex is the European Union's shared border agency. Today its work is mostly keeping people from entering the EU and helping send back those already inside it. This proposal would let it operate entirely outside Europe, helping one non-European country move people to another before they ever reach an EU border. A return hub is a facility in a third country where people removed from Europe are sent. This is a proposal, not a law, and the reporting rests on officials briefing Euronews rather than a published text.

**Why it matters:** It would push European migration enforcement physically outside European jurisdiction, where EU courts and asylum rules are hardest to bring to bear.

## 9. TECH

### Hackers post GMO Internet customer data after the Japanese host refuses to pay

GMO Internet told account holders to back up their files, shut down their computers and unplug them before noon Tuesday, after attackers who breached its Onamae.com Desktop Cloud service threatened to publish customer data and trigger ransomware at that hour. GMO found malware on customer servers on October 20 and began shutting the service down on October 21; it says as many as 26,739 servers may have been exposed, while cautioning that the figure is not a count of confirmed intrusions. It refused to pay, and material presented as customer data appeared online about 30 minutes after the deadline; GMO said no ransomware had been confirmed as of 1:55 p.m. local time. Japan's Financial Services Agency has reports of customer harm from five FX brokers. Accounts of the ransom demand conflict, from about $50 million down to about $1 million.

**In plain terms:** GMO runs Onamae.com, a service many Japanese businesses use to rent a Windows desktop that lives in GMO's data centre instead of on their own machine. It is popular with people running automated currency-trading programs. Attackers got into those rented desktops. The instruction to physically unplug matters because a remote desktop can reach back into the real computer connected to it, so an infection could spread the other way. Five brokerages have already reported customers losing money on trades they did not make.

**Why it matters:** The break-in hit the rented-desktop layer rather than any single company's network, which is how one intrusion becomes thousands of simultaneous victims.

## 10. TRUMP NEWS

### A federal judge rules the Justice Department's firing of Maurene Comey unlawful and orders back pay

Judge Jesse Furman of the Southern District of New York held on October 26 that the July 2025 removal of assistant US attorney Maurene Comey, a prosecutor on the Jeffrey Epstein and Sean Combs cases and the daughter of former FBI director James Comey, violated the Civil Service Reform Act and the Constitution's separation of powers. Furman wrote that such a prosecutor may be removed only for "such cause as will promote the efficiency of the service" and found the firing plainly failed that standard. He rejected the argument that Article II bars Congress from regulating removals, saying it would overturn nearly two centuries of Supreme Court precedent. He reinstated Comey retroactively with back pay through December 20, when she accepted another job, and did not reach her First Amendment claim. A Justice Department spokesperson said: "The Department disagrees with this decision and will appeal in due course."

**In plain terms:** An assistant US attorney is a career federal prosecutor, not a political appointee, and a 1978 law called the Civil Service Reform Act says such employees can be removed only for specific reasons and by specific procedures. The government argued that the president's authority over the executive branch lets him override that law. The judge disagreed. Comey has already taken another job, so the practical result is back pay plus a ruling on the record. The department says it will appeal, so this is a district-court decision, not a final one.

**Why it matters:** The ruling turns on the statute rather than the individual, holding that civil-service protections still bind the attorney general, which reaches every career prosecutor removed on the same theory.
