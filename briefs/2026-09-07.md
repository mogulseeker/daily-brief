## 1. AI INDUSTRY

### Lawsuits say OpenAI's safety team wanted police called on a future school shooter and was overruled

Thirty complaints filed in early September by survivors and families of February's mass shooting in Tumbler Ridge, British Columbia, which killed eight people and wounded 27, allege that OpenAI flagged the shooter's account for "gun violence activity and attack planning" about eight months earlier, that an internal safety team recommended referring her chat logs to law enforcement, and that company leaders vetoed the referral in June 2025 and only deactivated the account, after which she opened another. OpenAI says the activity did not meet its "imminent and credible risk" threshold and denies that policy chief Chris Lehane blocked a referral. Suits over alleged ChatGPT harms now number more than 50. None of the allegations has been tested in court.

**In plain terms:** Chatbot companies scan conversations and flag accounts that look dangerous, then have staff decide what happens next. The complaints say the flagging worked; what is disputed is what should have followed it. Closing an account costs the company nothing and involves no outsiders. Calling the police does, and can badly harm an innocent user. Each company currently makes that call privately, with no outside rule to follow. These are allegations, and OpenAI disputes them.

**Why it matters:** If a court ever rules on this, it sets the first legal standard for when an AI company must break a user's confidence and call the authorities, a duty that until now has been entirely voluntary.

### The AfD wins a German state election outright for the first time, and still cannot govern

The far-right Alternative for Germany won Saxony-Anhalt on September 6 with a preliminary 43.8 percent, more than double its 20.8 percent in 2021 and its best result in any German state vote; broadcaster exit polls had put it near 44.5 percent. Chancellor Friedrich Merz's CDU fell to 17.2 percent. The AfD took 39 of 83 seats, three short of a majority. Turnout was about 76 percent. Merz said the result "shakes our party to its very foundations" and that he was "deeply shocked." Elon Musk posted "Well done!"; lead candidate Ulrich Siegmund thanked him and offered cooperation. France's Europe minister, Benjamin Haddad, called it a "serious moment."

**In plain terms:** Germany's 16 states each elect their own parliament, and Saxony-Anhalt is a small eastern one. Every other German party has a standing pact, nicknamed the "firewall," never to govern with the AfD, which German domestic intelligence classifies as right-wing extremist. That is why finishing 26 points ahead still leaves the party out of power. The number to watch is three. Three more seats and the firewall would not have mattered, because an outright majority needs nobody's permission.

**Why it matters:** The firewall has held only because the AfD kept falling short of a majority, and the margin is now three seats in a state where the governing party lost more than half its support.

### Jaguar Land Rover cuts 4,000 jobs, about a tenth of its workforce

Britain's largest carmaker said Monday it will cut about 4,000 of its 43,000 jobs over two years, mostly at its UK head office, in a plan to save 1.7 billion pounds ($2.3 billion). It blamed US tariffs, competition from cheaper Chinese manufacturers, the cost of the electric-vehicle transition, and a cyberattack that halted production for more than a month. A voluntary redundancy window closes on October 4, and the company said compulsory cuts on less generous terms would follow if it falls short.

**In plain terms:** Jaguar Land Rover is owned by India's Tata Motors and builds Range Rovers in Britain for a global market, which exposes it to three squeezes at once. US import taxes make every car sold in its most profitable market dearer. Chinese electric-vehicle makers undercut it everywhere else. And going electric means paying for new plants while still earning from petrol models. The overlooked item is the hack: a month of stopped production costs about what a bad sales quarter does.

**Why it matters:** This is a tariff, a cyberattack and Chinese competition landing on one payroll at once, which is the combination now facing most of European manufacturing rather than one badly run company.

## 4. HEALTHCARE

### US measles cases hit 3,134, the highest count since 1991

The CDC reported 3,134 confirmed measles cases for 2026 as of September 3, across 47 jurisdictions and 38 separate outbreaks, with 93 percent of cases in people who were unvaccinated or of unknown vaccination status. Kindergarten MMR coverage fell to 92.4 percent in the 2025-26 school year from 95.2 percent in 2019-20. Pennsylvania's health department announced on August 25 that Lancaster County recorded the state's first two measles deaths in 35 years, both in unvaccinated people. The Pan American Health Organization has delayed its review of US elimination status to November.

**In plain terms:** "Elimination" does not mean a disease is gone. It means a country has gone 12 months without the virus spreading continuously inside its own borders, so every case traces back to someone infected abroad. The US has held that status since 2000. Measles needs roughly 95 percent of a community vaccinated to stop spreading, which is why a drop of under three points among kindergartners matters so much. Below that line it stops burning out on its own.

**Why it matters:** Losing elimination status would reverse a US public-health achievement that took four decades to build, and the vaccination rate driving it is still falling.

## 7. WILDCARD - HEALTHCARE

### Drug pricing deals now cover 89 percent of the branded market, three weeks before a tariff deadline

The White House announced on August 31 that nine more manufacturers - Alcon, Astellas, BeOne Medicines, BridgeBio, CSL, Kyowa Kirin, Sun Pharma, Teva and UCB - signed "most favored nation" pricing agreements, bringing the total to 26 companies covering 89 percent of the branded drug market. Medicaid pays the lowest price the manufacturer charges across a set of peer countries, and the nine committed nearly $20 billion in US manufacturing investment. Signers avoid Section 232 pharmaceutical tariffs of 100 percent until January 2029; those tariffs hit everyone else on September 29. Analysts quoted by STAT called the deals midterm messaging.

**In plain terms:** Americans have long paid more than Europeans for identical brand-name drugs. "Most favored nation" pricing means the US government pays whatever the lowest rich-country price is. The mechanism is not a law but leverage: sign, or face a 100 percent import tax on your patented drugs. That is why 26 companies signed within months. The limit analysts point to is scope - these deals mostly bind Medicaid, so someone on an employer plan may see nothing change.

**Why it matters:** Tariff leverage has reset pricing across most of the branded drug market without Congress passing anything, which means a future administration can unwind it the same way.

## 9. TECH

### A judge refuses to break up Google's ad business and rewrites its auction rules instead

US District Judge Leonie Brinkema rejected all three structural remedies the Justice Department sought in its ad-tech monopolisation case on September 2, including forcing Google to sell its AdX exchange. She imposed behavioural remedies instead: Google must make real-time AdX bid amounts available to rival ad servers, will retire its Unified Pricing Rules, and must let publishers set different price floors for individual bidders in Google Ad Manager. Her written opinion is sealed for 14 days, with redaction motions due September 16 and a jointly proposed final judgment due October 2.

**In plain terms:** Selling a website's ad space involves three pieces of software: one the publisher uses to offer the space, one advertisers bid through, and an exchange that runs the auction between them. Google owns all three, which the court found illegal. The Justice Department wanted the exchange sold off. The judge refused, and ordered Google to show rivals the incoming bids and let publishers price per bidder instead. That matters because behavioural orders need years of policing, while a sale happens once.

**Why it matters:** After a decade of antitrust cases against big technology firms, courts have now twice declined to break one up, which sets the practical ceiling on what the government can win.
