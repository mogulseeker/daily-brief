## 1. AI INDUSTRY

### Microsoft says its own new chip, not Nvidia's, will run most of its AI inference

Microsoft announced the Azure Maia 300 on Monday, October 12, its third-generation in-house AI accelerator, and said the Maia family will become the primary inference system for its own products and services, with multi-gigawatt deployments starting in 2027. Microsoft claims 40% more AI tokens per megawatt than leading GPU-based systems and 2.5 times the memory bandwidth of the Maia 200, with 96 chips per rack. No third party had tested the chip as of Monday, and Nvidia remains its supplier for training large models.

**In plain terms:** Running an AI model has two phases. Training is the expensive one-time schooling. Inference is answering each question you type, which happens billions of times a day. Microsoft rents Nvidia chips for both and wants to own the second half, because inference is where the recurring electricity bill lives. The 40% efficiency figure is Microsoft's own measurement, and no outside lab has checked it. Nothing deploys until 2027.

**Why it matters:** Inference is the recurring cost in AI, so a hyperscaler moving it onto in-house silicon shrinks Nvidia's most predictable future revenue rather than its current orders.

### The UK formally blames Iran's Revolutionary Guard for a plot against the base US bombers fly from

Prime Minister Andy Burnham told the House of Commons on Monday, October 12, that an attempted attack on RAF Fairford, the English airfield the US Air Force uses for its heavy bombers, was “highly likely” coordinated in Iran and “almost certainly” directed by the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps — Britain's first official attribution to Tehran. Seven men were arrested, all have been released on bail and none charged, and investigators found gasoline but no explosives. The Pentagon has moved every American bomber at the base back to the United States; Iran denies involvement.

**In plain terms:** RAF Fairford is a British airfield the US uses to park heavy bombers within reach of Europe and the Middle East. Someone allegedly tried to attack it. Britain says Iran's Revolutionary Guard, the branch of Iran's military that runs operations abroad, ordered it, which is a formal accusation between governments rather than a police finding. Note the gap: seven men were arrested and then bailed without charges, so the criminal case is far thinner than the political accusation.

**Why it matters:** Flying US bombers out of Britain is a concrete loss of forward basing, which says Washington treated the threat as credible even though no one has been charged.

**Why it matters:** Korea's two memory makers have become the pivot of the global AI trade, so an unverified Chinese engineering claim now moves world markets faster than an earnings report does.

## 4. HEALTHCARE

### Bristol Myers Squibb's lung-scarring drug succeeds at the lower dose and misses at the higher one

Bristol Myers Squibb said on Monday, October 12, that the Phase 3 ALOFT-IPF trial of admilparant in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis met its primary endpoint at the 60 mg dose, a statistically significant improvement over placebo in change in forced vital capacity at week 52. At 120 mg the drug slowed the decline in lung function but missed statistical significance. The trial enrolled about 1,255 adults, discontinuations from adverse events matched placebo, and the company has not released the size of the benefit.

**In plain terms:** Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis is scarring of the lungs with no known cause. The lungs stiffen, hold less air, and most patients die within three to five years of diagnosis. The two approved drugs slow the scarring without stopping it, and both are hard to tolerate. Admilparant blocks a receptor called LPA1 that helps drive the scarring. The strange part is that the lower dose cleared the statistical bar and the double dose did not, which can mean the higher dose is genuinely no better, or only that the trial was too small to show it.

**Why it matters:** No new drug mechanism has been approved for this disease in over a decade, so a Phase 3 win counts even before anyone has seen how large the benefit is.

### Nvidia's share of China's AI chip market falls to 34% as Huawei reaches 28%

Nvidia shipped 678,000 AI accelerators in mainland China in the first half of 2026, down 44% from a year earlier, cutting its share of the country's data-center AI chip market to 34%, according to IDC data reported on Tuesday, October 13. Huawei shipped 572,000, up from more than 300,000, for a 28% share. GPU shipments in China fell 26% to 991,000 units while other AI accelerators rose nearly 80% to 1.016 million, passing GPUs by volume. IDC credited US export controls and stronger domestic competitors.

**In plain terms:** Washington bars Nvidia from selling its best AI chips to China, on the theory that this slows Chinese AI down. The data show something more specific. Chinese buyers did not stop buying, they bought Huawei instead, and for the first time they bought more non-GPU accelerators than GPUs. IDC did not say which Nvidia products make up the remaining 34%, and some banned top-end chips are known to reach China through smuggling, so the real split is blurrier than the numbers look.

**Why it matters:** Export controls were meant to deny China computing power, and the first-half numbers show they mostly transferred market share to Huawei instead.

## 8. WILDCARD - HEALTHCARE

### An alpha-emitting cancer drug wins the first late-stage trial of its kind

Bristol Myers Squibb said on Monday, October 12, that a planned interim analysis of the Phase 3 ACTION-1 trial showed RYZ101 significantly improved progression-free survival, by blinded independent central review, in patients with somatostatin-receptor-positive gastroenteropancreatic neuroendocrine tumors that had progressed after lutetium-177 therapy. Controls received the investigator's choice of high-dose somatostatin analogues, everolimus or sunitinib. The company says these are the first Phase 3 results for an actinium-225 radiopharmaceutical in any indication, and has not disclosed the hazard ratio.

**In plain terms:** A radiopharmaceutical is a radioactive atom attached to a molecule that sticks to tumor cells, so the radiation is delivered from inside the body. The versions already approved use lutetium-177, which emits beta particles that travel a relatively long way. RYZ101 uses actinium-225, whose heavier alpha particles stop within a few cell widths but do far more damage where they land. This is the first time that approach has won a Phase 3 trial, and it did so in patients whose tumors had already outlasted the beta version. No numbers are public yet, so the size of the gain is unknown.

**Why it matters:** Actinium-225 is in severe short supply worldwide, so a first Phase 3 win turns isotope production into the bottleneck on an entire class of cancer drugs.

### A cyberattack takes Ahold Delhaize's grocery websites offline across the US and Europe

Ahold Delhaize, the Dutch owner of Food Lion, Hannaford, Stop & Shop, Giant Food and The Giant Company, took systems offline on Monday, October 12, after detecting what it called a cybersecurity incident, knocking out all five US chains' websites and apps and suspending online ordering and delivery. European sites including Albert Heijn, Delhaize and bol.com were also hit. Stores remain open with registers and refrigeration unaffected, and the company has not said what caused the incident, whether customer data was taken, or when service returns.

**In plain terms:** A grocery chain runs two businesses on one network: the shops, and the website that takes delivery orders. When something gets inside that network, the standard response is to pull the connected parts offline before it spreads, which is why the websites went dark while the registers kept ringing. So the outage reflects the company's own containment decision, not necessarily how far the attack reached. What it has not said is the revealing part: no cause, no answer on customer data, no restore date.

**Why it matters:** Online ordering collapsing at five US chains at once shows how much of grocery retail now runs on one shared corporate network rather than inside each store.

### Trump asks the Supreme Court to erase the $937,989 a court made him pay for “frivolous” arguments

Trump filed a petition for certiorari on Friday, October 9, after Justice Clarence Thomas granted two deadline extensions, asking the Supreme Court to revive his 2022 racketeering suit against Hillary Clinton and James Comey and to void the sanctions against him; SCOTUSblog reported the filing on Monday, October 12. Judge Donald Middlebrooks dismissed that suit in September 2022 and ordered Trump, his lawyer Alina Habba and her firm to pay $937,989.39 in fees and costs. On November 26, 2025, an Eleventh Circuit panel led by Chief Judge William Pryor affirmed both the dismissal and the sanctions in Trump v. Clinton, No. 22-13410, writing that “many of Trump's and Habba's legal arguments were indeed frivolous”; the full court denied rehearing in May 2026. The petition asks whether his filing deadline should have paused while he was president, and whether courts may sanction a president and his lawyers for raising that argument.

**In plain terms:** Trump sued Clinton and others in 2022, claiming a conspiracy behind the 2016 Russia allegations. The judge threw the case out and then ordered Trump and his lawyer to pay the other side's legal bills, which is what courts do when arguments are not merely losing but baseless. A federal appeals court agreed, in writing, and the full appeals court refused to revisit it. Trump now asks the Supreme Court to rule that holding the presidency should have paused his deadline to sue, and that a president's lawyers should not be punished for arguing so. Four justices must agree just to hear it.

**Why it matters:** The question presented asks the Court to carve out a sanctions exception for presidents, which is a bigger claim than the lawsuit it is attached to.
