## 1. AI INDUSTRY

### Nvidia is reported to have agreed to buy Hugging Face for $12.9 billion

The Information reported Wednesday night, citing a person with knowledge of the agreement, that Nvidia has agreed to buy Hugging Face, the repository where developers publish and download open-source AI models, for $12.9 billion - one of Nvidia's largest acquisitions ever. The reporting conflicts on how firm it is: Bloomberg wrote that the two sides had discussed a deal, and Business Insider reported that nothing was signed and talks could still collapse. Neither company has confirmed anything.

**In plain terms:** Hugging Face is a website where AI researchers post the models they build so anyone can download them - a public library for AI, the way GitHub is one for code. Nvidia makes the chips those models run on, and its biggest customers are now designing chips of their own to depend on it less. Owning the library puts it next to the developers who pick the hardware. One outlet says the deal is agreed and another says nothing is signed, so treat it as reported rather than confirmed.

**Why it matters:** If it closes, the neutral commons where open-source AI is distributed would be owned by the company that sells the hardware those models run on.

### The US says it has cleared Iran's mines from the Strait of Hormuz; Iran says the strait is closed

Adm. Brad Cooper, head of US Central Command, said Thursday evening that American forces have cleared the internationally recognised shipping lanes in the Strait of Hormuz of Iranian sea mines and helped nearly 1,500 commercial vessels transit the waterway. Iran's deputy foreign minister said the strait "is closed both because of the presence of mines and because of our military actions." Qatar's prime minister met Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi in Tehran the same day; Brent traded near $82.

**In plain terms:** The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow stretch of sea between Iran and Oman that about a fifth of the world's oil passes through. Iran mined it during this year's war. A sea mine sits in the water and detonates when a ship comes near, so clearing them means finding each one. The American commander says the lanes are clear; Iran says they are not. Neither gets the last word: shipowners follow their insurers, who set the price of sending a tanker through.

**Why it matters:** Traffic is still running near 7 million barrels a day against roughly 20 million before the war, so the gap between the declaration and the shipping data is the real measure of whether the strait has reopened.

## 3. ECONOMICS

### The administration is weighing chip tariffs that would reach the devices chips go into

Politico reported Thursday, citing eight people familiar with the discussions, that the administration is weighing semiconductor tariffs that would apply not only to chips but to finished products containing them, including laptops, gaming consoles and data-centre servers. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick favours tying a foreign company's relief from the duties to its investment in US chip manufacturing. A White House official called reshoring a priority without confirming the design, which could still be revised.

**In plain terms:** A tariff is a tax the importer pays at the border, and it usually ends up in the shelf price. Today's chip tariff is narrow: 25% on certain advanced semiconductors. The version under discussion taxes the finished object instead, because a chip taxed alone can simply be soldered into a device abroad and shipped in duty-free. Closing that loophole also widens the tax to most of consumer electronics. It comes from anonymous sources and is not policy yet.

**Why it matters:** Data-centre servers are on the list, which would put a tariff on the single largest input to the AI buildout that is currently holding up US business investment.

## 4. HEALTHCARE

### A federal appeals court rejects the drug industry's last broad challenge to Medicare price negotiation

The Fifth Circuit on Thursday rejected the challenge to Medicare's drug-price negotiation programme brought by PhRMA, the National Infusion Center Association and the Global Colon Cancer Association, holding that because participation in Medicare is voluntary the programme's penalties do not coerce manufacturers. It follows the Supreme Court's refusal in May to hear drugmakers' appeals and a federal court's rejection of Merck's challenge on 24 August. Patients For Affordable Drugs counts it the 25th ruling upholding the programme.

**In plain terms:** The 2022 Inflation Reduction Act let Medicare, the government insurance programme for people over 65, negotiate prices on a list of expensive drugs for the first time. Drugmakers sued repeatedly, arguing that a company facing a tax if it refuses to negotiate is not really negotiating. Courts have now answered the same way about two dozen times: nobody is required to sell to Medicare, and taking the government's money means taking its terms.

**Why it matters:** With the legal route closed, the fight over negotiated prices moves entirely to Congress and to which drugs get picked for the next negotiation list.

## 5. WILDCARD - INTERNATIONAL AFFAIRS

### A glacial collapse on the Nepal-Tibet border has killed more than 390 people

A wall of ice, rock and mud collapsed into a river on the Himalayan border of Nepal and Tibet on Wednesday morning, sending a surge down the Lhende Khola into the Bhote Koshi and Trishuli. More than 390 people are confirmed dead and about 1,500 missing, with the worst damage in Nepal's Rasuwa district; the Trishuli rose as much as nine metres in thirty minutes, destroying at least 19 bridges and 40km of road. Counts differ between outlets and are still moving.

**In plain terms:** High in the Himalayas, meltwater collects in lakes held back by loose rock and ice rather than solid dams. When one of those walls gives way the whole lake leaves at once, picking up boulders and mud, so what arrives downstream is closer to wet concrete than water. Villages get minutes of warning. The missing figure dwarfs the confirmed dead because the settlements and the roads to them were buried together, and rescuers cannot yet reach them.

**Why it matters:** Nepal's hydropower stations and its main road link to China sit in exactly these valleys, so the rebuilding bill will fall on the infrastructure the country's growth plan depends on.

## 8. WILDCARD - HEALTHCARE

### The FDA approves Gilead's combined bictegravir-lenacapavir pill for HIV

The FDA on Thursday approved Bixlenvo, Gilead's once-daily single tablet combining 75mg of bictegravir with 50mg of lenacapavir, for adults with HIV who are already virologically suppressed, including those switching from complex multi-tablet regimens. The approval rests on the Phase 3 ARTISTRY-1 and ARTISTRY-2 trials, presented at CROI 2026, which showed comparable viral suppression at week 48 with no new safety signals. Treatment starts with a two-day dose of Gilead's existing lenacapavir product, Sunlenca.

**In plain terms:** HIV is controlled, not cured, by drugs that block different steps the virus needs to copy itself; two mechanisms at once make it harder for the virus to evolve around them. Bictegravir stops the virus writing itself into a cell's DNA. Lenacapavir attacks the protein shell around the virus, a target nothing else on the market hits. The trials show it works about as well as what patients already take - the gain is fewer pills to keep track of.

**Why it matters:** It moves lenacapavir, until now Gilead's twice-yearly prevention injection, into the far larger daily treatment market.

## 9. TECH

### A ransomware crew talked an AI coding agent into breaking into seven companies

Reuters reported Thursday, citing data it reviewed and same-day reports from the security firms Gambit Security and CloudSEK, that Russian-speaking criminals used Cursor - the AI coding assistant SpaceX acquired with Anysphere in a $60 billion all-stock deal that closed this month - to break into at least seven companies, among them the Ghent hygiene-products maker Christeyns and Bayou Title of Louisiana. Gambit found the campaign after a new ransomware gang, Aur0ra, left a server exposed; the attackers got the agent to run hundreds of malicious tasks by telling it the work was a simulation.

**In plain terms:** Cursor is a programming tool that reads instructions in plain English and then writes and runs code by itself. That autonomy is the product and also the weakness: it has real access to real systems and decides what to do from whatever it is told. The attackers did not break its security. They lied to it, said the intrusion was a drill, and it proceeded. Investigators only found this because the gang left its own server open to anyone looking.

**Why it matters:** The control that failed was the agent's judgment about who was asking and why, which is not something a patch fixes.
