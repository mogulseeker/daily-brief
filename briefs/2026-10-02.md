## 1. AI INDUSTRY

### OpenAI fires three safety researchers over an alleged leak to an outside safety group

OpenAI terminated three researchers working on safety and alignment, the Wall Street Journal reported on October 1, after finding they had shared confidential information, some of it about the company's infrastructure architecture, with a third-party AI-safety organization. The three are Tomek Korbak, Jasmine Wang and Mikita Balesni. A spokesperson said they "mishandled sensitive information outside established company procedures"; OpenAI has not named the outside group or said what was shared, and the three have not commented publicly.

**In plain terms:** Alignment research checks whether an AI system does what its builders intended rather than something unexpected. The people doing that job often talk to outside safety groups, because an outside check only works if outsiders can see something real. OpenAI says these three went further and handed over confidential material, including how its computing systems are built. It has not said what was shared or with whom, so the only account of the facts is OpenAI's own.

**Why it matters:** Internal safety teams are the main early-warning system regulators are currently relying on, and a firing over disclosure sets the price of talking to outsiders without settling where the line between a leak and a disclosure sits.

## 2. INTERNATIONAL AFFAIRS

### Ethiopia and Eritrea cut diplomatic ties as drones strike the Ethiopian capital

Ethiopia said on October 1 it was expelling 10 Eritrean diplomats who "pose a direct threat to Ethiopia's national security" and closing its embassy in Asmara; Eritrea severed relations hours later. Overnight, three diplomatic sources told Reuters, explosions hit Addis Ababa near the national defense force headquarters and in the central 4 Kilo district, which an opposition-linked source said were drone strikes by the Ethiopian Peoples' Forces Alliance for Survival. Addis Ababa accuses Eritrea, Sudan and Egypt of backing the Tigray People's Liberation Front, which launched an offensive last week; all three deny it.

**In plain terms:** Ethiopia and Eritrea fought a border war from 1998 to 2000 that killed tens of thousands, then made peace in 2018. That peace has now formally ended. The new fighting is in Tigray, the northern region that fought the federal government from 2020 to 2022, and Eritrea sits on its border. What is new is the reach: the capital is hundreds of kilometres from the front. The drone claim comes from one opposition-linked source and is unverified.

**Why it matters:** Two states that fought a war and then made peace have now formally unmade it while a third force is striking the capital, which removes the diplomatic channel either side would need to stop a wider regional war.

## 3. ECONOMICS

### The dollar hits a 16-month high as long-term borrowing costs reach levels last seen in 2002

The dollar index rose to 101.848 on October 1, a 16-month high, while the 10-year Treasury yield touched 5.342% and the 30-year reached 5.683%, the highest since April 2002. Traders cut the odds of an October Federal Reserve rate increase to roughly one in three after softer August inflation data and dovish remarks from New York Fed President John Williams, but futures still price close to a 90% chance of a hike by year-end. The September jobs report, forecast at 84,000 to 90,000 jobs added with unemployment at 4.1%, was due at 8:30am Eastern on Friday.

**In plain terms:** A Treasury yield is what the US government pays to borrow, and it sets the floor for what everyone else pays, mortgages included. It is now at a level last seen in 2002, so two decades of cheap borrowing have ended. The cause is not panic but strength: the economy keeps running hot, so investors expect more rate rises, and money flows to the dollar to earn them. Friday's jobs number could move all of it.

**Why it matters:** The long end of the curve is pricing a higher cost of money for years, not months, which reprices every leveraged asset from housing to the debt-funded AI data-center buildout regardless of what the Fed does in October.

## 4. HEALTHCARE

### Humana drops Medicare Advantage plans covering 600,000 people as 2027 costs shift to patients

Humana is not renewing Medicare Advantage plans covering about 600,000 members for 2027, roughly 8% of its 7.2 million enrollees, mostly individual HMOs and PPOs rated 3.5 stars or lower; it expects to recapture about 40% of them. New CMS landscape data shows average monthly premiums falling 16.5%, from $14.37 to $12.00, while the Part D deductible rises to $700 and the out-of-pocket drug cap to $2,400, with published plan data showing out-of-pocket maximums up about 10% and deductibles up about 30%.

**In plain terms:** Medicare Advantage is the version of Medicare run by private insurers, chosen by about half of people on Medicare. Insurers are losing money on it and are fixing their margins two ways. They are shutting their worst-performing plans, which forces those members to shop again. And they are holding the monthly premium low, which is what shoppers compare, while raising the deductible and the out-of-pocket ceiling, which you only meet once you are sick.

**Why it matters:** A falling average premium alongside a 30% higher drug deductible is a transfer of cost from healthy enrollees to sick ones, and it lands in the same open-enrollment window where the premium is the number most people compare.

*Sources: [Healthcare Dive](https://www.healthcaredive.com/news/medicare-advantage-cuts-2027-cms-landscape/831563/) · [Becker's Hospital Review](https://www.beckershospitalreview.com/finance/humana-to-exit-medicare-advantage-plans-covering-600000-members-in-2027/) · [CMS](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/press-releases/medicare-advantage-medicare-prescription-drug-programs-expected-remain-stable-2027)*

Lula is the veteran left-wing president seeking a fourth term. His likely opponent is the son of the right-wing president he beat in 2022. A two-point gap with a three-point margin of error cannot tell you who leads, only that the race is even.

**Why it matters:** A near-tie heading into a runoff in a country with recent experience of a contested result means the margin, not the winner, is the thing to watch on Sunday night.

### Anthropic aims to start marketing its IPO on November 9 at a valuation near $2 trillion

Anthropic plans to begin marketing its initial public offering as soon as the week of November 9 and to start trading before Thanksgiving, Bloomberg reported on October 1, citing people familiar with the plans; investors put the valuation at $1.8 trillion to $2 trillion and the raise near $100 billion, which would be the largest IPO on record. The S-1 prospectus Reuters reviewed in late September showed 2025 revenue of about $4.6 billion against a $42 billion net loss, $518 billion in projected spending on cloud, compute and infrastructure, and a Founder LLC holding a single share carrying 50.1% of voting power.

**In plain terms:** An IPO is when a private company first sells shares to the public. Two numbers in Anthropic's own filing sit oddly together: it took in about $4.6 billion last year and lost $42 billion, because building and running these models costs far more than customers pay. The $518 billion spending plan is a bet that scale closes the gap. The Class F share means public investors supply the money without being able to outvote the founders.

**Why it matters:** A $2 trillion listing built on a $42 billion annual loss would make the public markets, rather than private investors, the thing holding up AI capital spending.

## 8. WILDCARD - HEALTHCARE

### Roche drug cuts breast cancer progression risk by 44% in results published in the NEJM

Roche published phase 3 results on October 1 in the New England Journal of Medicine showing that giredestrant, an experimental oral drug, plus everolimus cut the risk of disease progression or death by 44% against standard hormone therapy plus everolimus. Median progression-free survival was 8.8 months versus 5.5 months; among patients whose tumors carry an ESR1 mutation the risk reduction was 62% and median progression-free survival was 10.0 months. The evERA trial enrolled people with ER-positive advanced breast cancer that had worsened after a CDK4/6 inhibitor. Giredestrant is not yet approved.

**In plain terms:** Most breast cancers grow because estrogen feeds them, so standard treatment blocks that signal. Over time tumors route around the block, often by mutating the estrogen receptor itself, and the options run short. Giredestrant destroys the receptor rather than blocking it, which is why it still works on the mutated version, and that is where the trial's effect was largest. The gain is real but modest, about three extra months, and no regulator has approved it.

**Why it matters:** The effect is concentrated in ESR1-mutated patients, which pushes this class toward routine genetic testing at relapse rather than a one-size-fits-all second-line treatment.

## 9. TECHNOLOGY

### Judge clears about 5,000 publishers to pursue $3.2 billion from Google over ad tech

US District Judge P. Kevin Castel denied Google's motions for summary judgment on news publishers' main antitrust claims in an 88-page opinion issued September 30, sending the case toward a jury trial that has not been scheduled. The ruling lets Gannett's USA Today, the Daily Mail and a class of roughly 5,000 publishers pursue more than $3.2 billion in claimed damages, and declined to exclude the expert testimony behind those figures. The publishers allege Google's control of its publisher ad server, its AdX exchange and its auction rules cut their revenue or imposed excessive fees; no damages have been awarded.

**In plain terms:** When a news site shows you an ad, Google often runs the software the publisher uses to offer the space, the exchange where it is auctioned, and the tool advertisers bid with. Publishers say owning all three let Google set the rules and take a bigger cut. This ruling does not decide that. It says a jury can hear the claim and the damage estimates, which is usually the point where defendants settle. US antitrust damages can be tripled.

**Why it matters:** Private damages suits, not regulators, are now the mechanism most likely to put a price on Google's ad-tech conduct, and a jury date gives roughly 5,000 publishers leverage no agency remedy has produced.

*Sources: [Courthouse News](https://www.courthousenews.com/judge-hands-google-partial-defeat-in-ad-tech-monopoly-case/) · [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-01/google-to-face-3-2-billion-damage-claims-over-ad-tech-monopoly) · [Editor & Publisher](https://www.editorandpublisher.com/stories/publishers-clear-major-hurdle-in-google-ad-tech-antitrust-fight,263806)*

## 10. TRUMP NEWS

### Judge rules Trump unlawfully fired a US attorney the courts had appointed less than an hour earlier

US District Judge Stanley Bastian ruled from the bench on October 1 that the administration unlawfully removed Roger Rogoff as US attorney for the Western District of Washington, reinstated him immediately and barred his removal until a Senate-confirmed replacement is in place. Seattle's district judges had appointed Rogoff in July under the statute that lets courts fill a vacant US attorney post; the administration fired him less than an hour later. Government lawyers said they will appeal. Separately, in a TIME interview published October 1, Trump said he regretted nominating Justices Gorsuch, Kavanaugh and Barrett, saying "They voted against me too often" and "I gave them the chance of a lifetime, and it's unfortunate what they did." Asked whether he wanted loyalty from them, he said "No, I don't want loyalty. I want good decisions."

**In plain terms:** When a US attorney's job is vacant and the Senate has confirmed nobody, federal law lets the local district judges appoint someone. The Seattle judges did that in July; the administration fired their pick within the hour. The judge has now ruled it could not, because a prosecutor the court chose is not the president's to remove at will. The TIME quotes are from the same day: the justices voted against him too often, and he does not want loyalty.

**Why it matters:** Two different courts' appointment powers are now the live constraint, and the administration's own appointees on the Supreme Court are the ones Trump named as the problem.
