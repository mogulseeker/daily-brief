## 1. AI INDUSTRY

### Big Tech's AI spending faces a cash-flow test this week after Meta's first negative quarter since 2012

Microsoft and Alphabet report Wednesday and Amazon on Thursday, the first broad read on AI capital spending since Meta's October 21 results, where capital expenditure of $34.91 billion exceeded $32.79 billion of operating cash flow and left free cash flow at negative $2.12 billion, sending the stock down 7 percent. Microsoft has guided to more than $50 billion of capex this quarter and Azure growth of 44 to 45 percent; Alphabet's 2026 capex guidance is $195 billion to $205 billion. Credit markets moved first: CoreWeave's five-year credit default swaps have traded above 1,000 basis points, generally read as distress.

**In plain terms:** Capital expenditure, or capex, is money spent on buildings and equipment rather than on running the business day to day. The big cloud companies are spending it on data centers full of AI chips. Free cash flow is what is left over after that spending, and Meta's went negative for the first time since it listed in 2012, meaning the quarter's build cost more than the business generated. Credit default swaps are insurance against a company failing to pay its debts; CoreWeave's now cost over 10 percent a year, which is what lenders charge when they think default is a real possibility.

**Why it matters:** The question this week is not whether AI revenue is growing but whether the companies financing the build-out can keep paying for it out of cash flow instead of debt.

**In plain terms:** Brazil elects its president in two rounds: if nobody wins a majority outright, the top two meet again two weeks later. Lula lost the first round and won the second, which had never happened there before. Flavio Bolsonaro is a senator and the son of the previous right-wing president, who is himself barred from running. He is not accepting the count and wants a court to cancel it, partly because voting was held open two extra hours. Investors had been positioned for him to win, which is why Brazilian shares fell so hard when he did not.

**Why it matters:** The same electoral court that just certified the result now has to rule on a challenge to it, which determines whether this dispute stays in the courts or moves into the street.

**In plain terms:** Oil trades on expectations, so a diplomatic trip can move the price more than a day's actual production does. The Fed's difficulty is that war-driven fuel prices raise inflation without adding any growth.

**Why it matters:** Energy is the main channel through which the Iran war reaches American households, so what the Fed says about it on Wednesday matters more than whether it moves the rate.

## 4. HEALTHCARE

### ACA open enrollment starts November 1 with a 15 percent median premium increase and no enhanced subsidies

Open enrollment for 2027 Affordable Care Act coverage begins Sunday, November 1 and runs through January 15 on HealthCare.gov. KFF's review of preliminary filings from 276 insurers across all 50 states and the District of Columbia found a median proposed increase of about 15 percent, a second straight double-digit year after a 20 percent median finalized increase for 2026, with roughly four percentage points attributed to a sicker remaining risk pool. The enhanced premium tax credits expired at the end of 2025, restoring the cliff at 400 percent of the federal poverty level, above which the subsidy is zero; individual-market enrollment has already fallen 18 percent, from 25.2 million in June 2025 to 20.5 million in June 2026.

**In plain terms:** The ACA marketplace is where people who cannot get insurance through a job buy it, with the government covering part of the premium based on income. Congress let the larger pandemic-era subsidies lapse, so above roughly four times the poverty line you now pay the entire premium yourself. Healthier people drop out first when that happens, which leaves insurers covering a sicker group and raising prices on everyone who stays. Insurers say that pattern alone accounts for about a quarter of next year's increase. Around 4.6 million people have already left the market.

**Why it matters:** Each round of increases makes the remaining pool sicker and the next increase larger, which is the mechanism that unwinds an insurance market rather than merely shrinking it.

### Serbia's Vucic concedes to a student-led party list after the highest turnout since 1992

Aleksandar Vucic conceded on Sunday night that the Student List had won Serbia's snap parliamentary election, saying a sample of votes put it at 135 to 140 of the 250 seats against about 105 for his own list. Euronews reported the Student List on 52 percent to his 32.3 percent. Pollsters CeSID and Ipsos put turnout at 67.6 percent by 7pm, the highest since Yugoslavia broke up in 1992. The movement grew out of nearly two years of protests that began when a railway station canopy collapsed in Novi Sad in 2024, killing 16 people. Vucic blamed independent Serbian media, unnamed European countries and an “American deep state”; final official results were still pending.

**In plain terms:** Vucic has run Serbia for 14 years, first as prime minister and then as president, with his party controlling parliament throughout. In 2024 a concrete canopy at a train station collapsed and killed 16 people; students treated it as proof that corruption had become lethal and protested for nearly two years until he called an early election. They then ran their own list of candidates and, by his own count of the sample, won a majority of seats. He has conceded, though the official tally is not finished and he is disputing why he lost rather than whether he did.

**Why it matters:** Protest movements rarely convert street pressure into a parliamentary majority, and this one did it in a country both Moscow and Brussels have treated as winnable.

## 8. WILDCARD - AI INDUSTRY

### Texas suspends 17 data-center permits and shuts the portal that issued them during a state pause

The Texas Commission on Environmental Quality has suspended at least 17 stormwater construction permits tied to data-center projects and taken its STEERS online filing system offline. Watchdog groups FracTracker Alliance and Public Citizen published reports on October 19 and 20 counting 35 TCEQ permitting actions on data-center projects after Governor Greg Abbott's September 21 directive ordering the agency to issue no data-center permits until grid operator ERCOT completes an audit, due in mid-December. A TCEQ spokesperson said all notices of intent related to data centers have been suspended along with the online application system. Abbott's press secretary said “No permits have been approved,” and the agency says processing a filing is not approving it.

**In plain terms:** Before building a large facility in Texas you need a permit confirming that rain running off the site will not pollute waterways. Much of that was handled by a self-service portal that authorised filings automatically, with no official reading them. The governor had told the agency to stop permitting data centers while the grid operator worked out whether the state has the electricity to run them, but the portal kept authorising filings anyway, so the agency has now switched it off and pulled 17 authorisations back. The state's position is that nothing was ever formally approved.

**Why it matters:** The binding constraint on AI build-out is shifting from chips to state permitting and power, and Texas has just shown it will reverse approvals after the fact.

## 9. TECH

### Ukraine signs a letter of intent with Nokia and ICEYE for a satellite network it would own outright

Ukraine's Defence Ministry signed a letter of intent on October 22 with Nokia and the Finnish satellite maker ICEYE covering a secure satellite communications system the Ukrainian government would own and operate, including satellites, ground stations and user terminals. It was signed during Finnish Prime Minister Petteri Orpo's visit to Kyiv, one of seven documents worth about 300 million euros, and no timeline or funding has been announced for the satellite system itself. The move follows SpaceX speed limits on Starlink that Ukrainian officials said were still cutting connections for drones and aircraft moving faster than 100mph as of October 24; Chad Anderson of Space Capital said a working alternative is “two to three years away.”

**In plain terms:** Ukraine's drones and front-line units route their data through Starlink, SpaceX's satellite network, which means one company can degrade them by changing a setting. Ukraine wants a network it owns instead. ICEYE already builds small radar satellites and Nokia builds the ground network equipment, so the pieces exist. But what was signed is a letter of intent, which commits both sides to keep talking rather than to build: no money and no date are attached to the satellite part, and an industry analyst puts a usable alternative two to three years out.

**Why it matters:** Governments are beginning to treat commercially owned satellite links as a sovereignty problem rather than a procurement decision, which opens a market Starlink has had largely to itself.
