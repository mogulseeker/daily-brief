## AI INDUSTRY

### Anthropic tells IPO investors its models may soon cross its own line for self-accelerating research

Anthropic filed an amended S-1/A with the SEC on Monday adding a risk disclosure that its internal estimates suggest it may imminently meet its Responsible Scaling Policy definition of AI-driven R&D acceleration - roughly, as much AI progress in one year as two years would produce without it. The filing states the company has already used AI to build models whose capability jumps it did not recognize at the time, and that it is weighing policy changes requiring human review of certain R&D decisions. No model has crossed the threshold. Reuters and IFR have reported listing talks at $1.5 trillion to $2 trillion, which Anthropic has not confirmed.

**In plain terms:** Before selling shares to the public, a company must write down everything that could go wrong, and it can be sued for leaving something out. Anthropic used that section to say its own software is close to doing much of the work of building the next version of itself. Its measuring stick is a doubling: one year of progress where two would normally be needed. The uncomfortable part is the admission that some capability jumps were only recognized afterwards.

**Why it matters:** The brake on how fast AI improves has been assumed to be chips and engineers, and this is the first time a frontier lab has told securities regulators, under legal liability, that the brake may be coming off.

## INTERNATIONAL AFFAIRS

### US strikes Iranian targets on Qeshm Island hours after Trump said a deal was close

Three US officials told CBS News the military carried out limited, defensive strikes on Iranian targets in the Strait of Hormuz on Monday, hitting naval and mine-laying vessels. Iranian state media reported explosions on Qeshm Island, with further blasts heard at Bandar Lengeh. Axios and Fox News reported the strikes answered an IRGC missile fired at a US aircraft over the strait. A US official said this was not the start of expanded strikes. The strikes came hours after Trump signaled progress in Qatari-mediated talks, and on the eve of the midterm elections he had said would precede any new action.

**In plain terms:** The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow sea passage that about a fifth of the world's oil normally moves through, and Iran has been mining it. Mine-laying ships are the tool that keeps it shut, so hitting them is aimed at the closure rather than at Iran broadly. Both governments are describing this as narrow and defensive, which is how each avoids having to escalate. Talks in Doha are continuing without a breakthrough.

**Why it matters:** Washington is now shooting at the ships enforcing the blockade while negotiating to have it lifted, which prices the strait's reopening as a military outcome rather than only a diplomatic one.

## ECONOMICS

### US factories grew for a tenth month, but input prices stayed near two-decade highs

The ISM Manufacturing PMI rose to 55.5 in October from 54.5 in September, beating a consensus near 54.6 and marking the tenth consecutive month of expansion. Production jumped to 59.0, its highest since November 2021, and all five subindexes that feed the headline were in growth. The prices index eased to 75.2 from 77.9 but has now been above 70 for nine straight months. ISM's Susan Spence attributed the pressure to steel and aluminum prices, tariffs on imported goods, and petroleum-based inputs driven by the Middle East conflict. Two-thirds of survey comments were negative.

**In plain terms:** This survey asks purchasing managers whether things got better or worse than last month; above 50 means more said better. Output is strong, but the prices line tracks what factories pay for materials, and it has been running hot for nine months. That combination - healthy demand and stubborn input costs - is the one that argues against interest-rate cuts. The negative comments show managers are growing while unhappy about what growth is costing them.

**Why it matters:** A strong economy with sticky input inflation removes the Federal Reserve's easiest excuse to cut, which is why some officials have begun talking about hiking instead.

## HEALTHCARE

### FDA takes up the first gene therapy that could slow Huntington's disease

uniQure said Monday the FDA accepted its biologics license application for AMT-130, or ifezuntirgene inilparvovec, under priority review, setting a May 1, 2027 action date. Filed through the accelerated approval pathway, it would be the first disease-modifying treatment for Huntington's disease. The application rests on a 36-month Phase I/II analysis of 12 high-dose patients compared with matched external controls drawn from the ENROLL-HD natural-history database. The FDA had previously said it no longer agreed that data was adequate as primary evidence, then reversed. A confirmatory trial of roughly 200 patients, randomized 2:1, has been agreed.

**In plain terms:** Huntington's is an inherited disease that destroys brain cells and currently has no treatment that slows it, only drugs for symptoms. AMT-130 is injected into the brain once and is designed to quiet the faulty gene permanently. Notice the evidence base: twelve treated patients, compared not against a placebo group but against records of other patients tracked over time. That is a small, unusual foundation, which is why the FDA has floated an advisory committee and required a proper randomized trial afterwards.

**Why it matters:** Acceptance is not approval, but it signals the FDA will weigh twelve patients and historical controls as the basis for a first-in-class approval, which every rare-disease developer will now plan around.

## WILDCARD - AI INDUSTRY

### South Korea budgets 4.7 trillion won for a state-built frontier AI model

President Lee Jae Myung's 2027 budget speech on Monday pledged 4.7 trillion won, about $3.5 billion, to build a sovereign frontier AI model that he said would have the "world's highest performance." The figure sits inside a record 820.9 trillion won budget, up 12.8 percent, which also carries 21.3 trillion won for semiconductors, physical AI and AI data centers. The Ministry of Science and ICT plans to pick a lead developer by competitive tender once parliament passes the budget in December, with a winner possible by February and work starting in March 2027.

**In plain terms:** A frontier model is one of the handful of largest AI systems in the world, and today they are all built by American or Chinese companies. Seoul is proposing to fund one itself, combining state equity with private money and pointing the country's chips, data and researchers at a single project. The catch is that none of this is law yet: parliament votes in December, and the tender only opens afterwards.

**Why it matters:** Middle-sized economies are concluding that renting AI from foreign firms is a strategic dependency rather than a procurement decision, and are willing to spend multiple billions to avoid it.

## WILDCARD - ECONOMICS

### Apple blames memory-chip costs for a margin squeeze, and the AI selloff continues

Apple reported fiscal fourth-quarter revenue and earnings above estimates after Monday's close but guided gross margin to 45-46 percent, down from 47.9 percent in the September quarter, citing rising memory prices and higher AI research spending. The Nasdaq fell 1.1 percent on Monday and the S&P 500 lost 0.5 percent, while the Dow rose 0.4 percent as cheaper energy lifted cyclicals. The Nasdaq had already dropped 5.3 percent in October, its worst month since March, after Alphabet and Amazon results renewed concerns that AI infrastructure spending is outrunning near-term cash generation.

**In plain terms:** Memory chips are the storage inside a phone or a server, and AI data centers buy them in enormous quantities. When those buyers compete for supply, the price rises for everyone, including the company putting memory in iPhones. So Apple, which is not building giant AI data centers, is paying for other companies' decision to build them. That is the AI boom showing up as a cost on the income statement of a firm that is not in it.

**Why it matters:** The AI buildout has stopped being a story about who gains and started being a story about who pays, and the bill is now landing on companies that made no AI capital commitments at all.

## WILDCARD - INTERNATIONAL AFFAIRS

### India's voter-roll revision has removed about 130 million names, and Delhi is protesting

Thousands marched in Delhi on Sunday demanding the resignation of India's chief election commissioner over the Special Intensive Revision of electoral rolls, which has deleted roughly 130 million names and is expected to leave a register of about 880 million voters. Opposition leader Rahul Gandhi warned Prime Minister Narendra Modi, Home Minister Amit Shah and the election chief of legal action. The Election Commission says the exercise removes duplicate, deceased and ineligible entries and that eligible people may apply for reinstatement. Delhi police had earlier removed Jantar Mantar, the city's long-standing protest site, from its approved list.

**In plain terms:** India periodically cleans its voter list, striking people who have died, moved or registered twice. This round struck about 130 million names at once, which is more people than live in Japan. The dispute is not whether cleaning is legitimate but whether the method catches real voters, and the burden falls on each struck person to notice and reapply. Claims that removals targeted particular communities are contested and not established.

**Why it matters:** The scale means that even a small error rate disenfranchises millions in the world's largest electorate, and the remedy requires the affected voter to act before the next election rather than the state to prove the deletion was correct.

## WILDCARD - HEALTHCARE

### Senators give ten ACA insurers until Friday to explain the removal of 760,000 enrollees

Thirteen Senate Democrats led by Finance Committee ranking member Ron Wyden, with Bernie Sanders among the signatories, wrote to the chief executives of the ten largest ACA marketplace insurers - including UnitedHealthcare, Cigna, Elevance, Centene, Oscar and Molina - demanding answers by November 6. The letters ask why insurers sent unsolicited messages asking enrollees to verify Social Security numbers and then handed CMS lists of those who did not respond, who were terminated in September as "phantom enrollees." The senators ask how many children were on those lists. CMS has reinstated an unspecified number of improper terminations.

**In plain terms:** To cancel someone's subsidized health plan, the government needs to identify them. Insurers sent verification requests, and people who did not reply - because the message went to a stale address, or looked like spam - were listed to the government and dropped. The senators are asking whether the insurers checked what legal authority CMS was acting under before handing over the names. The insurers have not yet responded publicly.

**Why it matters:** The practical effect is visible in hospital accounts already: Community Health Systems posted an $86 million quarterly loss and raised its 2026 estimate for exchange disenrollment and self-pay shifts to $80-90 million from $50-75 million.

## TECH

### FBI names North Korea for the $6.3 billion Binance theft, the largest crypto heist recorded

The FBI issued an alert attributing the theft of more than $6 billion in virtual assets from Binance, on or about October 29, to TraderTraitor, a North Korean state group, and published over 600 blockchain addresses tied to the laundering. Reports put the total near $6.3 billion, the largest cryptocurrency theft on record, ahead of the same group's $1.5 billion Bybit hack in February 2025. The FBI says the stolen assets are being converted mainly into Ether. North Korea called the allegation "absurd rhetoric" on Tuesday. Binance customers have withdrawn $4.64 billion since withdrawals resumed; the exchange says it will cover the losses.

**In plain terms:** Crypto exchanges hold customers' coins the way a bank holds deposits, except that a successful theft is final because there is no central authority to reverse it. North Korea has industrialized this: the money is not stolen for a single payday but funds the state, which is why the same group keeps appearing. Publishing the 600 addresses is an attempt to make the coins unspendable by asking every exchange to refuse them.

**Why it matters:** A single theft worth more than North Korea's estimated annual export earnings means sanctions enforcement now depends on commercial exchanges choosing to blacklist wallets, not on governments.

## TRUMP NEWS

### The Supreme Court threw out the ballot request in hours, without asking California to reply

On Monday the Supreme Court denied the Justice Department's emergency application to force California to set aside about 20,000 mail ballots from people the administration says may be noncitizens. The unsigned order reads: "The application for injunction pending appeal presented to Justice Kagan and by her referred to the Court is denied." The Court ruled within hours and did not request a response from California. District Judge David Carter had earlier refused a restraining order, describing the federal process for identifying those voters as "rife with uncertainty and errors," and the Ninth Circuit declined to intervene 2-1. The same day, District Judge Myong Joun dismissed the administration's suit seeking five years of Harvard admissions records, finding the Justice and Education Departments had not taken the procedural steps required before filing. California Attorney General Rob Bonta called the Supreme Court denial "completely appropriate, very expected."

**In plain terms:** An emergency application asks the Supreme Court to act before the normal appeal finishes. Courts usually ask the other side to file a reply first; here they did not, which means the justices did not think the argument needed one. The underlying problem was the list itself - a district judge found the method for identifying the 20,000 people produced errors - so the case failed on its evidence before reaching any question about noncitizen voting. The Harvard dismissal was also procedural, and the government may refile.

**Why it matters:** Two of the losses turned on the government not doing the required paperwork first, which is a complaint about the Justice Department's lawyering rather than about the courts it has been attacking.
