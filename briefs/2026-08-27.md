## 1. AI INDUSTRY

### Nvidia reports a $96.2 billion quarter and guides to $108 billion

Nvidia said revenue for the quarter ended July 26 was $96.2 billion, up 106% from a year earlier and 18% from the prior quarter, with data-center revenue at a record $89.0 billion, up 117% year over year. Non-GAAP earnings were $2.22 a share against $1.05 a year ago, and gross margin held at 75.0%. The company guided to $108 billion in the current quarter, plus or minus 2%. Shares rose about 7.6% in after-hours trading.

**In plain terms:** Nvidia makes the chips that nearly every AI system is trained and run on, so its sales are the closest thing there is to a meter on what the world is actually spending to build AI. Almost all of the money - $89 billion of the $96 billion - came from selling equipment to data centers, the warehouses of computers where AI models live. Revenue doubled in a year, and the company told investors to expect another $12 billion jump next quarter. The number worth watching is the 75% gross margin: Nvidia still keeps three-quarters of every dollar, which means nobody has yet forced it to cut prices.

**Why it matters:** The guidance implies buyers have already committed next quarter's money, which pushes the question of whether AI capital spending is sustainable out at least another six months.

## 2. INTERNATIONAL AFFAIRS

### Iran and Oman agree a temporary shipping corridor through the Strait of Hormuz

Iranian deputy foreign minister Kazem Gharibabadi said the two countries reached an understanding on a temporary joint navigation corridor through the strait and a joint mine-clearing project, after talks in Tehran between foreign ministers Abbas Araghchi and Badr Albusaidi. Inbound traffic would run entirely through Iranian territorial waters and outbound traffic partly through Omani waters; military vessels are excluded. The sides have 30 to 60 days to negotiate a permanent scheme. Brent fell about 3% to below $86, a one-month low. Qatar's prime minister, Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman al-Thani, arrived in Tehran on Thursday to try to restart US-Iran talks.

**In plain terms:** The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow sea passage at the mouth of the Persian Gulf that roughly a fifth of the world's oil has to travel through. It has been effectively closed since the US-Israel war with Iran began in February, which is why oil has been expensive all year. This understanding does not end the war or reopen the strait outright - it carves out a temporary lane, and the inbound half of that lane runs through Iranian waters, meaning Iran controls the traffic. The unsettled part is money: Iran has been asking for transit fees worth 5% to 7% of each cargo, Oman has argued for about 3%.

**Why it matters:** Routing the corridor through Iranian waters hands Tehran formal control over global oil traffic that six months of US strikes were meant to take away.

## 3. ECONOMICS

### US inflation held at 3.7% in July, two days before the Fed chair's first Jackson Hole speech

The Bureau of Economic Analysis reported that the PCE price index rose 3.7% in the year to July, a tenth of a point above consensus, while core PCE, which strips out food and energy, held at 3.3% and rose 0.2% on the month. Both are unchanged from June and well above the Fed's 2% target. Futures markets put roughly a one-in-three chance on a rate move at the September meeting, with the debate over whether to raise rather than cut. Chair Kevin Warsh gives his first Jackson Hole keynote on Friday at 10am ET.

**In plain terms:** PCE is the price gauge the Federal Reserve actually watches when it sets interest rates - it tracks what Americans really spend on, and adjusts as people switch to cheaper products. It has now sat at 3.7% for two months running, and that is the problem: the target is 2%, and progress has stopped rather than merely slowed. That is why traders are pricing a chance of a rate increase rather than a cut, the opposite of where the argument stood a year ago. Warsh took the job in May and has not yet said publicly which way he leans.

**Why it matters:** A Fed that has to weigh hiking in an election autumn removes the cheap-money assumption sitting underneath both equity valuations and the administration's growth forecasts.

## 4. HEALTHCARE

### FDA approves the first broad RAS-targeting drug for metastatic pancreatic cancer

The FDA approved daraxonrasib, sold as Rasonque by Revolution Medicines, for adults with metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma who have had at least one prior systemic therapy or cannot take multi-drug chemotherapy. In the randomized Phase 3 RASolute 302 trial of 500 previously treated patients, median overall survival was 13.2 months on the drug against 6.7 months on standard chemotherapy. The once-daily oral tablet inhibits multiple forms of the RAS protein and does not require a companion diagnostic test.

**In plain terms:** RAS is a protein switch inside cells that tells them when to grow; in most pancreatic cancers it is stuck in the on position, which is what makes the disease so aggressive. Drugmakers spent forty years failing to block it, because the protein has almost no pocket for a drug molecule to grab onto. This one blocks several mutated versions at once, which is why patients do not need a genetic test first to find out whether they qualify. Roughly doubling median survival, from about seven months to thirteen, is not a cure - in this disease it is the largest gain in decades.

**Why it matters:** A targeted cancer drug that works without a companion diagnostic sidesteps the genetic-testing bottleneck that has kept precision medicines from reaching community oncology practices.

## 5. WILDCARD - AI INDUSTRY

### OpenAI publishes benchmarks claiming its own chip beats Nvidia hardware on efficiency

OpenAI released performance figures for Jalapeño, the inference processor it designed with Broadcom and unveiled in June, showing higher single-user token throughput and more AI processing per kilowatt than a reference Nvidia Blackwell system. OpenAI says the chip went from concept to tape-out in nine months, will be deployed across its own infrastructure by the end of 2026, and that second and third generations are in development. Analysts told CNBC the results are a threat to Nvidia's margins in inference, the fastest-growing part of the market.

**In plain terms:** There are two jobs in AI: training a model, which happens once and needs enormous computers, and inference, which is answering each user's question and happens billions of times a day. Inference is where the electricity bill lives, so a chip that does the same work on less power is worth a great deal of money. OpenAI did not build a general-purpose chip; it built one shaped around its own models, which is how it can beat a more flexible Nvidia part on this particular test. These are OpenAI's own benchmarks on hardware nobody outside the company has measured, so treat them as a claim rather than a verified result.

**Why it matters:** Nvidia's largest customers are now also its competitors on the half of the market that is growing fastest.

Sources: [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/26/openai-jalapeno-ai-chip-nvidia.html) · [Broadcom](https://investors.broadcom.com/news-releases/news-release-details/openai-and-broadcom-unveil-llm-optimized-intelligence-processor) · [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2201045/openai-broadcom-jalapeno-inference-processor-ai-accelerator/)

## 6. WILDCARD - INTERNATIONAL AFFAIRS

### Ukrainian drones halt processing at Russia's fourth-largest oil refinery

Ukraine's General Staff confirmed that drones struck the Lukoil-owned Nizhegorodnefteorgsintez refinery at Kstovo, in Nizhny Novgorod region, on the night of 25-26 August, starting a large fire. Reuters reported the plant halted oil processing; it handles about 17 million tonnes of crude a year and accounts for up to 11% of Russian petrol output, and supplies the Russian armed forces. It is at least the fifth Ukrainian strike on the site this year, after attacks on 5 April, 20 May, 24 June and 2 July.

**In plain terms:** A refinery turns crude oil into the things you can actually use: petrol, diesel, jet fuel. Russia has plenty of crude but far fewer places able to convert it, so knocking out one large refinery causes fuel shortages even though nothing has happened to the oil itself. This plant alone makes roughly one in nine litres of Russia's petrol. Ukraine has hit it four times already this year, which tells you both that the target matters and that Russia keeps getting it running again - the damage is real but so far repairable.

**Why it matters:** Repeated refinery damage squeezes Russian government revenue and domestic fuel supply at the same time, which is slower than sanctions but much harder to route around.

## 7. WILDCARD - HEALTHCARE

### A cyberattack has stopped Boston Scientific shipping orders worldwide

Boston Scientific disclosed in a securities filing on Wednesday that it identified unauthorized activity on Tuesday, 25 August, which caused a network outage and cut access to the systems it uses to process and ship customer orders. The medical device maker said the disruption is global; staff at its Irish plants were told to work from home. No group has claimed the attack, the company has not said whether the impact will be material, and it has given no restoration timeline. Shares fell about 5.8% in premarket trading.

**In plain terms:** Boston Scientific makes implanted devices - heart stents, pacemakers, catheters - that hospitals order as they need them rather than stockpiling. The attack did not damage the devices or the factories; it took out the ordering and shipping software, which in a just-in-time supply chain amounts to nearly the same thing. This is the second large medical-device company hit this month, after Baxter International had 7.1 million records leaked. The company has not said what kind of attack this was, so whether patient or hospital data was taken is still unknown.

**Why it matters:** Hospitals hold days rather than weeks of implantable device stock, so an outage measured in weeks stops being a corporate problem and becomes a clinical one.

## 8. WILDCARD - ECONOMICS

### Trump signs a proclamation opening 300,000 tonnes of low-tariff beef imports

The president signed a proclamation on Wednesday raising the in-quota volume for imported lean beef trimmings by 300,000 metric tons for 2026, to be released in three 100,000-tonne tranches from 1 September on a first-come, first-served basis. The White House fact sheet says the additional beef is expected to sell at about a 25% discount to prevailing import prices, and cites USDA forecasts of a roughly 4% fall in US beef production this year, attributing it to drought, wildfires, and restrictions on Mexican cattle imports imposed to block New World screwworm.

**In plain terms:** A tariff-rate quota lets a set volume of a product in at a low tariff and taxes everything above that at a high one. Lean beef trimmings are the imported lean meat blended with fattier American beef to make ground beef, so this quota sets the price of a hamburger fairly directly. US cattle numbers are down and beef prices have risen about 12% over the past year. One cause the White House lists is its own screwworm restriction on Mexican cattle, so the fix is to import more of the beef that trade barriers were designed to limit.

**Why it matters:** A tariff-rate quota is the one price lever a president can pull without Congress, which makes this the likely template if other food prices keep climbing into the midterms.

## 9. TECH

### SpaceX plans a $100 billion Starship launch site in coastal Louisiana

SpaceX and the state of Louisiana unveiled plans for Starbase Louisiana, a launch complex on about 125,000 acres at Pecan Island in Vermilion Parish, on the Gulf coast south of Lafayette. State officials put the investment at $100 billion and the build-out at ten Starship launch pads; Elon Musk said the site would eventually hold more than a dozen launch towers and support over 30 Starship flights a day. Construction is scheduled to start in 2027, with first launches no earlier than 2029. SpaceX has not published a spending breakdown, and reported figures for the initial facility vary.

**In plain terms:** Starship is the fully reusable rocket SpaceX is building to replace Falcon 9, its current workhorse. A reusable rocket is only cheap if you fly it constantly, and the existing Starbase in south Texas is hemmed in by a town, a wildlife refuge and a launch licence that caps flights per year. Louisiana marshland is empty, coastal, and lets rockets fly out over the Gulf. Treat the headline numbers as ambition rather than schedule: nothing launches there before 2029, and the $100 billion figure comes from state officials, not from SpaceX.

**Why it matters:** Launch cadence rather than rocket design is now the binding constraint on SpaceX's satellite, lunar and Mars plans, and cadence is decided by real estate and permits.

## 10. TRUMP NEWS

### Judge rules the Postal Service violated her mail-voting injunction, then dissolves the injunction a day later

US District Judge Indira Talwani ruled on Tuesday that the administration violated her nationwide injunction by finalizing the Postal Service rule implementing Trump's mail-voting executive order. She agreed with the plaintiffs, the League of Women Voters and the ACLU among them, that USPS had "feigned compliance," and rejected its argument that it had complied because the contested provision will not apply to the November midterms. On Wednesday she vacated that injunction without prejudice, citing the Supreme Court's 24 August order, decided 6-3, that lifted a separate injunction blocking parts of the same executive order in California and 22 other states. She did not hold anyone in contempt, and plaintiffs may seek a new injunction now that the rule is final.

**In plain terms:** An injunction is a court order telling someone to stop doing something while a case is argued. Talwani had ordered the Postal Service not to finish writing the mail-voting rule; it finished writing it anyway, then argued it had obeyed because the disputed part will not take effect until after November. She found that was not compliance. What makes the sequence odd is the ending: one day after finding the government broke her order, she had to dissolve that order, because the Supreme Court had just ruled for the administration in a related case.

**Why it matters:** The finding stays on the record even though the order it enforced does not, which is what a plaintiff will cite the next time this administration tells a court it complied.
