## 1. AI INDUSTRY

### OpenAI's revenue came in $20 billion below the number the market had been using

OpenAI told investors its annualized revenue was approaching $50 billion at the end of September, first reported by the Financial Times on October 8 and confirmed by CNBC, Reuters and The Information. A figure near $68 billion had circulated earlier in the autumn; people familiar with the matter said it included gross revenue booked by OpenAI's partners, while OpenAI counts net. The Nasdaq Composite closed down 1.25% at 27,193.34, its worst day in seven weeks, with Oracle off more than 5%.

**In plain terms:** OpenAI is privately held, so outsiders learn its finances only when it tells investors. Annualized revenue takes the latest month and multiplies it out, so it is a snapshot, not a year's takings. The catch is accounting: the $68 billion figure counted money passing through to partners, while $50 billion counts only what OpenAI keeps. Nothing about the company changed this week; the yardstick did.

**Why it matters:** Suppliers whose valuations rest on OpenAI's spending now have a smaller revenue base to point at, in the same week the company is arranging tens of billions in debt against future compute.

**Why it matters:** The committee attached the world's most visible award to a legal finding that both Washington and Jerusalem reject, which makes that finding harder to treat as a fringe position.

## 3. ECONOMICS

### Record tanker attacks in the Strait of Hormuz cut oil flows and pushed Brent back above $104

Kpler counted 10 tankers struck in the Strait of Hormuz between September 28 and October 4, above the previous weekly high of six since the US-Iran war began on February 28. Crude crossing the strait fell 27% week on week to at least 10.1 million barrels a day, about 74% of pre-war levels. Brent settled 4% higher at $104.28 on October 8, US retail diesel averaged $6.28 a gallon, and domestic diesel stocks ran more than 15% below a year earlier.

**In plain terms:** The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow sea passage at the mouth of the Gulf that much of the world's oil has to sail through. Ships still go, but fewer of them, and those that do are hit more often, so freight and insurance cost more. Diesel matters more than gasoline here: it moves trucks, trains and farm equipment, so its price turns up later in everything else.

**Why it matters:** Diesel above $6 with stocks 15% short of last year leaves no cushion for a cold snap or one more refinery outage, which is the path from an energy shock to a headline inflation print.

### Medicare's 2027 quality ratings handed Humana a reversal and cut UnitedHealth and CVS

CMS released the 2027 Medicare Advantage star ratings late on October 8. Humana said 95% of its members will be in plans rated four stars or better next year, up from 20% in 2026, after its largest contract, covering about 2.4 million members, rose from 3.5 stars to 4. Its shares gained roughly 15% in Friday premarket trading. J.P. Morgan estimates UnitedHealth's four-star-plus share will fall to about 67% from 81% and CVS Health's to about 70% from 84%.

**In plain terms:** Medicare pays private insurers to run coverage for older people and grades each plan one to five stars on things like whether members get recommended care. Four stars unlocks bonus payments worth billions across a large insurer. Humana spent a year chasing a few measures and moved one enormous contract over the line, which is why half a star shows up as a 15% share move. The UnitedHealth and CVS numbers are analyst estimates, not CMS totals.

**Why it matters:** Star ratings fund the extra benefits insurers advertise, so this reshuffle will surface next autumn as richer Humana plans and thinner UnitedHealth and CVS ones.

### Broadcom is in talks to borrow more than $50 billion so OpenAI can buy its chips

Broadcom is in early talks with lenders for a debt package of more than $50 billion to finance OpenAI's purchases of the custom AI accelerators the two companies are designing together, Bloomberg and the Wall Street Journal reported on October 7 and 8. Apollo and Blackstone are among the private-credit firms approached, the first tranche could be about $30 billion, and no agreement is expected before year-end. The package would mirror a roughly $60 billion facility Broadcom arranged for Anthropic.

**In plain terms:** A chip supplier would normally sell you chips and leave you to find the money. Here the supplier is arranging the loan too, because its customer's spending runs far ahead of its income. The cash comes from private credit funds rather than banks, so there is less public disclosure about who holds the risk. Watch the collateral: these deals are increasingly secured on the chips themselves. Nothing is signed yet.

**Why it matters:** When a vendor finances its own customer's purchases, reported demand and available credit stop being independent signals, so a funding squeeze and a demand drop would arrive as the same event.

## 7. WILDCARD - HEALTHCARE

### The drug industry sued to kill Medicare's new foreign-price benchmark for clinic drugs

PhRMA sued the administration on October 7 in the US District Court for the District of Columbia to vacate the GLOBE model, a CMS rule that from January 1, 2027 requires manufacturers to pay Medicare rebates on Part B drugs benchmarked to prices in 19 countries. The complaint names Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. and CMS Administrator Mehmet Oz, and argues the mandatory rebates "predetermine the outcome" rather than test a payment model. CMS projected $2.3 billion in annual savings at proposal and about $80 million a year in the final rule.

**In plain terms:** Medicare Part B covers drugs a doctor injects or infuses in a clinic, and the US pays far more for them than other wealthy countries. GLOBE makes drugmakers refund the gap against a basket of 19 countries' prices. The law CMS is using lets it run experiments on how Medicare pays, so the industry's sharpest argument is that a fixed formula applied to everyone is not an experiment.

**Why it matters:** If a court accepts that a mandatory nationwide rule cannot be an innovation model, it narrows the main authority both parties have used to change how Medicare pays for anything.

## 8. WILDCARD - AI INDUSTRY

### USA Today's publisher sued OpenAI over training on 19 newspapers

USA Today Co., formerly Gannett, and 13 affiliated entities sued OpenAI on October 8 in the US District Court for the Southern District of New York, case 1:26-cv-08892, seeking more than $250 million and a jury trial. The 79-page complaint names seven OpenAI entities and alleges the company copied hundreds of thousands of articles from 19 publications, including The Tennessean, IndyStar and The Columbus Dispatch. It asks the court to order the destruction of any GPT model trained on that journalism. Reuters first reported the filing.

**In plain terms:** A model learns to write by reading enormous amounts of text, and most of that text belongs to someone. Copyright law permits some reuse without permission, called fair use, and that is the whole fight. What makes this filing notable is the remedy: not only money but destruction of the trained models, because a model cannot forget one newspaper on request. The $250 million is a demand, not a judgment.

**Why it matters:** A publisher of local dailies suing as a bloc changes the arithmetic, because the plaintiff is no longer a single national title OpenAI can settle with quietly.

## 9. TECH

### The FCC will vote to bar every China-based lab from certifying electronics for the US market

The FCC said it will vote on October 29 on an order barring all laboratories in China and Hong Kong from testing and certifying devices such as phones, cameras and computers for US sale, widening a 2025 rule that reached only labs owned or directed by entities deemed security risks. It would also cover labs in countries that deny reciprocal access to US facilities, taking effect in December 2028. Chairman Brendan Carr said 82% of electronics were tested in China in 2025 against under 4% in the US; the Epoch Times reported the share as about 83% of certification applications and noted the FCC's own statement did not name China.

**In plain terms:** Before a gadget with a radio in it can be sold in America, a lab has to confirm it does not interfere with other devices, and the FCC decides which labs may do that. Almost all of that work happens in China because it is several times cheaper, roughly $400 to $1,300 against $3,000 to $4,000. Removing those labs does not block Chinese products; it moves a bottleneck.

**Why it matters:** Certification is a chokepoint nobody notices until it closes, and a shortage of testing slots delays product launches as effectively as a tariff raises prices.

Sources: [Reuters](https://www.yahoo.com/news/politics/articles/us-fcc-vote-ban-chinese-190209332.html) · [The Epoch Times](https://theepochtimes.com/us/fcc-proposal-would-end-chinese-labs-role-in-us-device-testing-6101028) · [Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.com.my/2026/10/08/us-fcc-to-vote-on-ban-of-chinese-labs-testing-us-electronics/)

### A judge Trump appointed extended his order forcing the White House to readmit CNN, MS NOW and Politico

Judge Timothy Kelly, whom Trump nominated in 2017 and the Senate confirmed 94-2, extended through Tuesday, October 13 his order requiring the White House to admit journalists from CNN, MS NOW and Politico, after a hearing on October 8. Kelly first blocked the ban on September 24, ordering officials to "immediately return, reinstate, and restore" hard passes stripped from 78 journalists around September 18. The administration said the reporting threatened national security and that the outlets failed to meet "professional standards"; Kelly called that rationale "so vague it hardly does the trick" and wrote that nothing in the record predating the suit suggested a national-security motive.

**In plain terms:** A White House hard pass is the badge that lets a reporter walk onto the grounds without an appointment. The administration took 78 of them from three outlets, and a judge the president himself appointed ordered them restored. Notice what is doing the work: not whether the coverage was fair, but that the government must give a reason for pulling a credential and then stick to it. The ruling is still temporary.

**Why it matters:** The docket now holds a Trump appointee's finding that the stated national-security justification appears nowhere before the lawsuit was filed, which is the kind of detail that decides the case on appeal.
