**In plain terms:** The F-35 is the most advanced combat aircraft the United States exports, and Washington controls tightly who may buy one. Selling 48 of the same jets to Saudi Arabia cuts against that, which is why this is a notification to Congress rather than a contract: lawmakers get a window to object.

**Why it matters:** The sale prices Saudi security cooperation in hardware that Washington has spent decades withholding from Arab states, and it puts the qualitative-edge commitment to Israel in front of Congress as a vote rather than a policy.

## 3. ECONOMICS

### Bank of Japan raises its policy rate to 1.25%, the highest in 31 years

The Bank of Japan voted 7-2 on Friday, September 18, to raise its target for the uncollateralized overnight call rate to around 1.25%, effective September 24 — Japan's highest policy rate since 1995 and its second hike of 2026, three months after the last rather than the six-month gaps kept since March 2024. Board members Toichiro Asada and Ayano Sato dissented. August headline inflation ran at 1.9% year on year, with core excluding fresh food easing to 1.7%. The yen weakened past 157 per dollar afterward.

**In plain terms:** Central banks raise rates to make borrowing costlier, which cools spending and, in theory, inflation. Japan is the odd case: it spent roughly thirty years fighting the opposite problem, prices that would not rise, so 1.25% is high by Japanese standards and low by everyone else's. The strange part is the currency. Higher rates usually attract buyers, but the yen fell — two dissents told traders the bank may not keep going, and what moves a currency is the expected path, not the step just taken.

**Why it matters:** A weaker yen after a hike tells you markets priced the move as the end of something rather than the start, which leaves the bank's inflation problem and Washington's pressure on it both unresolved.

## 4. HEALTHCARE

### South Korea moves to approve abortion pills, seven years after its ban was struck down

South Korea's government said this week it will approve a two-drug pack of mifepristone and misoprostol for use through the ninth week of pregnancy, targeting availability as early as the first quarter of 2027. For the first two years, doctors must prescribe and dispense the drug directly at medical institutions rather than through pharmacies. South Korea's Constitutional Court struck down the near-total abortion ban in 2019, but parliament never passed replacement legislation and no abortion medication has been approved in the seven years since.

**In plain terms:** Medication abortion means ending an early pregnancy with two pills rather than a procedure, and it is standard in most wealthy countries. South Korea's top court threw out the criminal ban in 2019, but a court can strike down a law without writing the one that replaces it, and lawmakers never did. That left a gap: abortion was no longer a crime, yet the pills were never licensed, so they were not legally available at all.

**Why it matters:** The restrictions written into the approval, not the approval itself, will determine whether access actually changes for women outside major cities.

Trump said the same day that if he judged the move “at all a hostile act, I will put very serious tariffs or stop trading with Europe on many things.” The US already charges a 50% tariff on Canadian goods; about 70% of Canadian exports go to the United States.

**In plain terms:** The European Union is a club with a fixed rulebook, and “associate member” is not a category in it — von der Leyen is proposing to invent one. Canada has said it does not want to join outright; it wants something deeper than a trade deal, covering defence manufacturing and technology. The reason is arithmetic: roughly seven in ten Canadian exports go to a single customer that now charges 50% tariffs on them.

**Why it matters:** If the EU can invent a membership tier for a country outside Europe, tariffs stop being a bilateral lever and start pushing partners into blocs that outlast the tariffs.

## 7. WILDCARD - HEALTHCARE

### Africa CDC keeps the Congo Ebola outbreak at emergency status as deaths pass 3,500

The Africa CDC Emergency Consultative Group completed an independent scientific review on Thursday, September 17, and recommended keeping the Public Health Emergency of Continental Security in place for the Bundibugyo ebolavirus outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. As of September 16 the DRC had reported 7,404 confirmed cases and 3,577 deaths, with Ituri province accounting for 5,751 cases and North Kivu for 1,323. Group chair Salim Abdool Karim said the evidence gives “grounds for cautious optimism, but it does not yet show consistent control across all affected areas.”

**In plain terms:** Ebola is not one virus but a family of them, and the vaccines and treatments that worked before were built against a different member of that family, the Zaire species. This outbreak is the Bundibugyo species, so the existing tools have to be tested rather than simply deployed, which is happening while the outbreak runs. That is the main reason the death toll far exceeds recent Congolese outbreaks.

**Why it matters:** Keeping the emergency designation is what holds international funding and staffing in place, and the review makes clear the tools that ended the last outbreaks do not yet apply to this one.

### China cut government spending again in August, deepening its fiscal pullback

A broad measure of Chinese budget spending fell 6.7% in August from a year earlier, a sharper drop than July's 4.4%, according to Bloomberg calculations using Ministry of Finance data released Friday, September 18. Broad revenue rose 2.9% over the same period. The withdrawal of support continues even as growth cools: August retail sales grew 0.4% year on year and fixed-asset investment contracted 7.2% for the year to date.

**In plain terms:** When private demand is weak, the standard government response is to spend more — on infrastructure, subsidies, anything that puts money into circulation. Beijing is doing the reverse, cutting faster in August than in July while consumer spending barely grew. Revenue rising at the same time makes it starker: the state is taking in more and putting out less, which drains money rather than adding it.

**Why it matters:** If China is tightening fiscally into a slowdown, the global disinflation that cheap Chinese goods have supplied gets stronger, not weaker, just as other central banks are still fighting inflation at home.

## 9. TECH

### Snap starts selling standalone AR glasses for $2,195

Snap opened sales of Specs, its first augmented-reality glasses aimed at consumers, on Wednesday, September 16, at $2,195 with a $200 refundable deposit and shipping to the US, UK and France this fall. They run without a phone or tethered compute puck, using two Qualcomm Snapdragon processors, and project in-lens overlays across a 51-degree field of view, which Snap compares to a 24-inch monitor seen from a desk. Battery life is about four hours in mixed use, with AI features supplied by OpenAI and Google.

**In plain terms:** Augmented reality means drawing images onto what you are already looking at, rather than replacing your view the way a VR headset does. The hard part has never been the display; it is fitting the computer, battery and cooling into something that sits on your nose. Most products cheat by sending the work to a phone or a box in your pocket. Snap says these do not, which is why they are heavy and cost $2,195.

**Why it matters:** Pricing a self-contained pair at $2,195 sets the number Apple and Meta have to beat, and reveals that the binding constraint on the category is battery and weight rather than optics.

The fight now is over the building itself, and the judge's answer was narrow: no emergency hearing, but thirty days' warning before anything comes down.

**Why it matters:** The order converts a rhetorical threat into a procedural one: any demolition now leaves a thirty-day paper trail in a case where the court has already found one injunction violated.
