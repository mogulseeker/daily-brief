## 1. AI INDUSTRY

### Sony and Warner's publishing arms sue Anthropic over songs used in training

Sony Music Publishing and Warner Chappell Music, with other publishers, sued Anthropic on Friday, August 28, in the US District Court for the Northern District of California, alleging it trained Claude on "tens of thousands" of copyrighted compositions through what the complaint calls a "brazen campaign of illegally torrenting, scraping, and downloading copyrighted works." The publishing arms of all three major music companies are now litigating against Anthropic. The suit follows the company's $1.5 billion settlement with book authors over training data.

**In plain terms:** A music publisher owns the songwriting - the melody and the words - separately from the recording you actually hear, so these are the companies that own the songs rather than the tracks. They say Anthropic took their catalogs without paying, partly by pulling them off piracy networks, and fed them into the models behind Claude. Notice where the fight really sits: courts have been more open to the argument that training on a work can be fair use than to the argument that it was fine to obtain the work illegally in the first place. Anthropic has already paid $1.5 billion to settle a similar claim from book authors, and that number is the benchmark this case will be measured against.

**Why it matters:** The expensive question for AI labs is turning out to be not whether training is fair use, but how the training material was acquired.

## 2. INTERNATIONAL AFFAIRS

### US strikes Iranian launchers in the Strait of Hormuz; Iran fires missiles at US bases in Jordan

US Central Command said forces struck two Iranian rocket launchers on Larak Island on Sunday after Revolutionary Guard units were seen preparing to fire rockets carrying sea mines into the Strait of Hormuz - the first publicly confirmed American strike on Iran in about a month. Iranian state media named two dead, Hamid Avazzadeh and Ali Fayazi, and the Revolutionary Guards said soldiers and civilians were killed and wounded and promised "response and punishment." Early Monday they fired ballistic missiles at two bases hosting US forces in Jordan, reported to include Muwaffaq Salti Air Base near Azraq; Jordan's armed forces said they intercepted eight missiles that entered Jordanian airspace.

**In plain terms:** The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow sea passage at the mouth of the Persian Gulf that a large share of the world's oil is shipped through. Iran can effectively close it without sinking anything, by scattering sea mines that shipping companies then have to assume are there. The US says it destroyed the launchers before the mines went into the water, which is why this was two targets rather than a bombing campaign. Iran answered by firing at American bases in Jordan, a country that borders neither of them, and Jordan says it shot eight missiles down.

**Why it matters:** Six months into an on-and-off war, both sides are still choosing small strikes over escalation, but each round now runs through a third country's airspace.

## 3. ECONOMICS

### Brent crude tops $90 as the Gulf exchange of fire resumes

Brent crude rose about 2.9% on Monday to roughly $90.69 a barrel after the strikes, leaving it up about 8% over the past month and about 33% from a year ago. The Energy Information Administration has raised its 2026 forecasts to $87 average Brent and $3.78 a gallon for retail gasoline, up from $3.64. The move compounds an inflation problem the Federal Reserve has not solved: core PCE, its preferred gauge, ran at 3.3% year over year in the August 26 report, and market-implied odds of a September rate hike have climbed to roughly 60% since Chair Kevin Warsh's August 28 Jackson Hole remarks.

**In plain terms:** Crude oil is the input under almost every price in the economy, because everything that is grown, made or delivered has to move. When traders think a war might interrupt supply, they bid the price up before any barrel actually goes missing, which is why the price jumped the day after the strikes rather than after any shortage. That matters for interest rates: the Fed raises rates to cool demand, but an oil shock raises prices for reasons demand cannot explain. Markets have now shifted from expecting cuts to pricing a coin-flip chance of a hike next month.

**Why it matters:** An energy shock on top of 3.3% core inflation has flipped the market's base case from rate cuts to a possible hike, which reprices mortgages and corporate borrowing.

## 4. HEALTHCARE

### McKesson confirms a breach as an extortion group claims 284 million patient records

McKesson, the largest US pharmaceutical distributor, disclosed unauthorized access to third-party applications and data theft, saying it discovered the intrusion on August 25. The extortion group ShinyHunters told BleepingComputer it used voice-phishing calls to take over several employees' Okta single sign-on accounts, then pulled about 1TB out of the company's Salesforce and Snowflake environments between August 21 and 25, demanding $55,236,150 within 72 hours. It claims 284 million records - a count of database rows tied to tens of millions of patients, not 284 million people - including names, birth dates, Social Security numbers, Medicaid details, medical record numbers and medication and allergy information.

**In plain terms:** McKesson is not a hospital or an insurer. It is the middleman that moves a large share of America's prescription drugs and runs software that pharmacies and clinics depend on, so its systems hold records from many organizations at once. Nobody broke any encryption here: the attackers phoned employees, talked them out of their logins, and walked in through the company's own sign-on system. The 284 million figure is the attackers' claim and counts rows in a database, so the number of real people is smaller and still unknown. Unlike a credit card, a Social Security number or a diagnosis cannot be reissued.

**Why it matters:** The largest concentrations of US patient data sit with distributors and clearinghouses that patients have never heard of and cannot choose to avoid.

### DeepSeek is reported to be closing a $7.4 billion round at a $74 billion valuation

People familiar with the talks told Bloomberg and the South China Morning Post that DeepSeek is finishing a round of about 50 billion yuan, roughly $7.4 billion, at a pre-money valuation near 500 billion yuan, or about $74 billion, with a close targeted for the end of August. Returning backers named in the reporting include the venture funds Monolith and Shixiang Capital and the battery maker CATL. Proceeds are earmarked for model research and about a gigawatt of new computing capacity, ahead of a possible IPO filing this year and a 2027 debut on Shanghai's STAR Market. DeepSeek has not confirmed the terms.

**In plain terms:** DeepSeek is the Chinese lab that startled the industry in early 2025 by matching much richer American competitors at a fraction of their reported training cost. Until this year it took no outside money. A pre-money valuation is what investors agree the company is worth before their cash lands, so $7.4 billion going in at $74 billion means the new investors buy roughly a tenth of it. Most of the money is going into compute - the buildings, chips and electricity needed to train models - which is the constraint Chinese labs face, since US export rules limit which chips they can buy. Only the reporting is public here; the company has confirmed nothing, so treat the numbers as credible but unconfirmed.

**Why it matters:** A Shanghai listing would give China's leading model developer a public-market funding source that US export controls cannot reach.

## 6. WILDCARD - INTERNATIONAL AFFAIRS

### Iceland votes against reopening EU membership talks

Icelanders voted 52.8% to 47.2% against reopening the European Union accession negotiations that were suspended more than a decade ago, on 225,031 votes cast and 82.5% turnout. The referendum decided whether to restart talks, not membership itself. The campaign turned largely on fishing rights, the cost of living and control of natural resources; opponents argued EU membership would surrender authority over Iceland's fisheries. Prime Minister Kristrun Frostadottir had pledged that a no vote would take the EU question off the political agenda for the rest of her mandate, which runs to 2028.

**In plain terms:** Iceland has about 400,000 people and one dominant export industry: fish. Joining the EU would mean handing fishing quotas in Icelandic waters to a common European policy, which is the single thing most opponents would not accept, and it outweighed the argument that EU membership would lower prices for a country that imports nearly everything else. The margin was narrow - about 12,000 votes - but turnout was very high at 82.5%, so this is a clear reading of the country rather than a low-turnout accident. Because the question was only whether to restart talks, a no vote ends the process before it begins.

**Why it matters:** The EU's enlargement pitch just lost in one of the wealthiest and most obviously eligible candidate countries, on resource control rather than economics.

## 7. WILDCARD - HEALTHCARE

### The first targeted drug for pancreatic cancer reaches pharmacies at $39,800 a month

Revolution Medicines' daraxonrasib, sold as Rasonque, became available after an FDA approval on August 26 for previously treated metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma - the first RAS inhibitor cleared for the disease. In its phase 3 trial, patients on the oral drug lived a median of 13.2 months against 6.7 months on chemotherapy, a 60% reduction in the risk of death. The company set a wholesale list price of $39,800 for a 30-day supply, about $477,000 a year, and says it expects insurer discounts of roughly 20% to 30%. The company filed on July 22 and was cleared just over a month later using a priority review voucher.

**In plain terms:** RAS is a protein that acts like a stuck switch in most pancreatic tumors, telling cells to keep dividing. It was considered undruggable for forty years because its surface has no good pocket for a drug molecule to grip. This pill grips it. Doubling median survival from under seven months to just over thirteen is a large result in a cancer that kills most patients within a year, and it is still measured in months, not cures. The price is a list price, which almost nobody pays in full; what patients owe depends on insurance, and the company says it expects to discount it by a fifth to a third.

**Why it matters:** A drug class written off as impossible now has an approval in the hardest common cancer, which redirects investment across every RAS-driven tumor type.

## 8. WILDCARD - INTERNATIONAL AFFAIRS

### Nepal's flood death toll rises to 903, with more than 4,200 still missing

Nepal's National Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Authority put the toll from the August 26 Bhotekoshi and Trishuli flash floods at 903 on Monday, up from about 700 two days earlier, with roughly 4,247 people unaccounted for and 267 injured across eight districts. Chitwan has recovered the most bodies, 272, followed by Nawalparasi East with 207. More than 20,800 police, army and armed police personnel are searching. Scientists now attribute the flood to an ice-and-rock collapse that dammed the Lhende Khola, a tributary above the Bhotekoshi, forming a temporary lake that then burst; the collapse registered as a magnitude-5.2 seismic event.

**In plain terms:** High in the Himalayas a mass of ice and rock broke loose and fell across a small river, plugging it. Water piled up behind the plug, then the plug failed all at once and the whole lake came down the valley as a wall of water - which is why so many of the dead are in districts far downstream that never saw the mountain. The collapse was violent enough to register on earthquake sensors. Researchers say it will take time to measure how much warming contributed to this particular event, though warmer glaciers make these dam-burst floods more frequent.

**Why it matters:** The missing outnumber the confirmed dead more than four to one, and the hazard formed upstream of the border, where no Nepali monitoring station could have seen it.

## 9. TECH

### NASA's $4.3 billion Roman Space Telescope launches nine months ahead of schedule

The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope lifted off on a SpaceX Falcon Heavy from Launch Complex 39A at Kennedy Space Center at 7:26 a.m. Eastern on Sunday, separating 31 minutes later and deploying its solar panels. The observatory is bound for the Earth-Sun L2 point about a million miles out, near the James Webb Space Telescope, carrying a 300-megapixel infrared wide-field camera and a coronagraph. Over a five-year prime mission it is designed to survey more than two billion galaxies, measure dark energy and dark matter, and find on the order of 100,000 exoplanets. It launched roughly nine months early, which is rare for a NASA flagship.

**In plain terms:** Roman's camera sees a patch of sky about a hundred times wider than Hubble's at similar sharpness, and that changes the kind of question you can ask. Instead of studying a few objects closely, it can photograph billions of galaxies and measure how the universe's expansion has sped up over time - the effect physicists call dark energy and still cannot explain. Its coronagraph blocks a star's glare so a planet next to it becomes visible, the technique needed before anyone can photograph an Earth-like world directly. L2 is a point past the Moon where a spacecraft can sit in permanent shade, which keeps an infrared telescope cold enough to work.

**Why it matters:** A wide-field survey produces an open data set that thousands of researchers mine for a decade, so its return compounds in a way a single-target telescope's does not.

## 10. TRUMP NEWS

### Trump said Iran's main oil terminal was "being blown to smithereens" and posted AI-generated video; no strike there was evident

On Sunday, August 30, as US and Iranian forces exchanged fire in the Gulf, Trump published a one-line social media post saying Iran's Kharg Island was being "blown to smithereens," attached to AI-generated video clips depicting an aerial attack on what appears to be an oil facility. Reuters reported that it was not clear whether the post described a threat or an attack under way, and that several hours later there was no evidence Kharg had been struck. Kharg is the terminal through which Iran exports about 90% of its oil. The confirmed US action that day was Central Command's strike on two rocket launchers on Larak Island, a separate island near the Strait of Hormuz; CENTCOM made no claim about Kharg.

**In plain terms:** Kharg Island is a small island in the Persian Gulf where almost all of Iran's oil is loaded onto tankers, so destroying it would be one of the largest escalations available short of an invasion. The president wrote that it was being destroyed and attached video that was generated by software rather than filmed. Hours later reporters could find no sign that anything had happened there, and the US military's own account of the day described a much smaller strike on a different island. Oil traders price war risk off statements like this in real time; crude rose about 2.9% on Monday.

**Why it matters:** When a head of state illustrates a military claim with synthetic video, the people who have to price war risk lose the ability to treat official statements as evidence.
