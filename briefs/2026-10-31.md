**Why it matters:** The company that set the AI market's price expectations has now marked itself down, which removes the private anchor that public valuations were being justified against.

## HEALTHCARE

### Medicare drug plan premiums are projected to rise 50 percent for enrollees who stay put

A KFF analysis projects the enrollment-weighted average monthly premium for stand-alone Medicare Part D plans reaches $51 in 2027, up 50 percent from $34, for enrollees who keep their current plan. KFF finds no $0-premium stand-alone plans will be available to enrollees without low-income subsidies, that 10.4 million of them, 76 percent, would pay more by staying put, and that the average deductible rises to $643 from $540. The driver is CMS ending its temporary Part D Premium Stabilization Demonstration after 2026. CMS projects a far smaller move, to about $36 from $35.09, because its average spans the whole market.

**In plain terms:** Part D is the prescription-drug side of Medicare, sold by private insurers that each set their own monthly price. For several years the government paid insurers to hold those prices down; that payment ends after 2026, so prices are springing back. The two competing figures are not contradictory. KFF asks what you pay if you do nothing; the government averages across all plans, including cheap new ones.

**Why it matters:** A subsidy that expires quietly can take more out of household budgets than a law that passes loudly, and this one lands hardest on the enrollees least likely to shop.

### Big Tech's future AI commitments now exceed $3 trillion, most of it off the balance sheet

Bloomberg reported October 30 that Alphabet, Meta, Microsoft, Amazon and Oracle together hold more than $3 trillion in future commitments for data centres, leases, equipment and energy. About $1.3 trillion is future lease obligations, some running 30 years, and about $1.9 trillion is other purchase commitments. Alphabet's total exceeds $1 trillion and Meta's exceeds $800 billion, up from roughly $2.4 trillion in July for four of the companies, before Oracle was counted. Bloomberg notes the companies disclose these commitments differently, which makes direct comparison difficult.

**In plain terms:** A commitment is a promise to pay later, like signing a 30-year lease. It is not money already spent, so a company can look profitable now while owing enormous sums for years ahead. Across the five biggest AI spenders those promises pass $3 trillion. Each firm reports them differently, so the total is stitched together rather than official. It also explains why one weak quarter moves these shares so violently: the bills are locked in.

**Why it matters:** Locked-in obligations are what turn an investment cycle into a solvency question, because leases and energy contracts come due whether or not the demand that justified them arrives.

### ACA enrollment opens with benchmark premiums up 13 percent and eleven insurers leaving

Affordable Care Act open enrollment begins November 1 in most states for 2027 coverage. Benchmark exchange premiums rise an average of 13 percent, according to KFF, with insurers citing higher medical costs and the expiration of the enhanced federal subsidies at the end of 2025. Eleven insurers have announced plans to exit some state marketplaces for 2027, including Cigna in Tennessee. Enrollment has already fallen to roughly 19 million since the enhanced subsidies lapsed, and people earning at least 400 percent of the federal poverty level no longer qualify for assistance.

**In plain terms:** The ACA marketplace is where people without job-based insurance buy their own. The government covers part of the premium, and for several years it covered an unusually large part. That extra help ended, so buyers see more of the real price just as the price rises 13 percent. Insurers leaving states compounds it: fewer plans to switch to means shopping around stops working as an escape route.

**Why it matters:** Rising prices and departing insurers reinforce each other, and each turn of that loop leaves the marketplace covering a smaller and more expensive group.

## TECH

### The FCC moves to open 482 megahertz to satellite phone service and drop the rule that protected carriers

At its October 29 open meeting the Federal Communications Commission adopted a notice of proposed rulemaking that would make 482 megahertz of additional spectrum available for satellite-to-phone service, raising the eligible bands from five to thirteen. It would also remove the requirement that a satellite operator lease spectrum from every carrier holding those airwaves across an area as large as the continental United States, the provision that has kept direct-to-device service dependent on carrier cooperation. AT&T, Verizon and T-Mobile, through CTIA, had asked the commission to drop the 2.5 and 3.45 gigahertz bands, 290 of the 482 megahertz at issue. Final rules need a second vote after comment.

**In plain terms:** Spectrum is the set of radio frequencies phones use, and transmitting on any slice requires a government licence. Until now a satellite company wanting to reach ordinary phones directly had to rent frequencies from every mobile carrier in the area, which handed the carriers a veto. The FCC is proposing to remove that veto and roughly triple the available frequencies. This is a first vote, not a final rule, so nothing changes yet.

**Why it matters:** Dropping the carrier-lease requirement would turn satellite operators from partners that mobile networks sell through into competitors that sell around them.

### A judge opens a formal inquiry into whether Justice Department lawyers misled her about Trump's golf course plans

Judge Ana Reyes of the US District Court for the District of Columbia said on October 30 she will open a formal inquiry, and appoint a special master, into whether agency and Justice Department attorneys made misrepresentations to the court in the litigation over East Potomac Golf Links. Trump said publicly the course would close November 15 and renovation would begin; administration lawyers told Reyes there was no final plan. Per Bloomberg Law, Associate Attorney General Stanley Woodward told her at an October hearing that a planning submission had been in the works for weeks, which the department had not disclosed. Reyes extended her ban on tree removal, required 10 days' notice before closure, and ordered documents preserved. She stated she was not finding that the lawyers had lied, only that she intends to consider it.

**In plain terms:** When the government is sued, its lawyers owe the judge candour, and a judge who suspects they fell short can appoint an outside lawyer, a special master, to investigate. Here the president said the course was closing on a set date and work was starting; his own lawyers told the judge no plan was settled. One of those statements was wrong. The judge has not ruled that anyone lied, and said so explicitly.

**Why it matters:** A special-master inquiry into government lawyers' candour builds a sworn record of who knew what, which is harder to manage than simply losing the underlying case.
