### The four biggest AI developers testified under oath for the first time, and their own former researchers testified alongside them

Senior policy staff from Anthropic, OpenAI, Google and Meta gave sworn testimony to the New York City Council on October 5, the first time the four largest AI developers have appeared together under oath before a legislative body. Three former researchers testified the same day. Jacob Coxon, formerly of Anthropic, said that “on the current path, I think it is more likely than not that humanity loses control to these AIs, and it could end in human extinction.” Google's Alice Friend asked instead for a “comprehensive” federal framework.

**In plain terms:** A city council cannot regulate an AI company — New York's cannot fine OpenAI or stop a model shipping. What it can do is put people under oath, where answers carry legal weight. That is what made this unusual: the companies had to answer in a room where their own former colleagues said the work could end badly. Google asked in return for one national rulebook, which would also override the state and city rules now being drafted.

**Why it matters:** State and city hearings are becoming the main venue where frontier labs answer questions on the record, because the administration's national AI framework directs agencies to preempt state rules through federal litigation.

## 2. INTERNATIONAL AFFAIRS

### Germany arrested the former head of its foreign intelligence service on suspicion of treason

Federal prosecutors arrested August Hanning, 80, on October 6 on suspicion of treasonous espionage, spying out state secrets and attempted treason. Hanning ran Germany's foreign intelligence service, the BND, from 1998 to 2005. His former chief of staff, named only as Manfred D., who held that post under several later BND presidents until March 2026, was arrested for aiding and abetting. Prosecutors allege the two agreed in spring 2010 that Manfred D. would pass BND material to Hanning for payment, and that roughly 2,000 documents changed hands, including material on Iran's nuclear program and the Russian military. Investigators say Hanning used some of it in an analysis prepared for a foreign intelligence service they have not named, and that the case is not believed to involve Russia.

**In plain terms:** The BND is Germany's equivalent of the CIA, and Hanning directed it for seven years. The allegation is not that he spied in office. It is that after leaving he kept a source inside the building, his old chief of staff, and used what that source sent him in paid consulting work. The scope is the damaging part: an alleged pipeline running sixteen years, carrying intelligence partner countries shared on the understanding it stayed inside the BND.

**Why it matters:** Allied services share material on the assumption it cannot leak back out through a retired director's consulting practice, so the arrest is as much a problem for what Germany's partners will hand over next as for German law.

## 3. ECONOMICS

### The New York Fed has been inside four big banks' books examining what they lent to private credit firms

Semafor reported on October 5 that New York Fed supervisors have visited JPMorgan, Wells Fargo, Barclays and Morgan Stanley since the spring, asking about each bank's total exposure to private credit funds, its risk management and the quality of the collateral behind those loans. Bank lending to nonbank financial firms has grown from about $300 billion in 2016 to more than $1.5 trillion, roughly 11% of all bank loans. The review was prompted in part by JPMorgan's March markdown of private credit loans to software companies facing AI competition. The Fed and all four banks declined to comment.

**In plain terms:** Private credit funds lend to companies banks will not lend to directly. The banks did not step back, though. They lend to the funds instead, keeping the risky loan off their own books while the money still comes from them. That chain is now worth over $1.5 trillion. The trigger is the interesting part: JPMorgan's markdowns hit software firms, written down because AI is taking their business. Semafor has this exclusively, so treat it as credible but unconfirmed elsewhere.

**Why it matters:** Supervisors asking about collateral quality rather than exposure size is the step that usually precedes capital requirements, and the trigger was AI disruption showing up as loan losses rather than as a stock story.

## 4. HEALTHCARE

### CMS finalized new price transparency rules and cut how often insurers must report prices

CMS announced final rules on October 5 rewriting how hospitals and health plans disclose what care costs. Insurers will post one in-network rate file per provider network instead of one per plan, must give enrollees personalized cost-sharing estimates by telephone as well as online, and face new accountability for incomplete or inaccurate data. The same rule cuts reporting frequency for some pricing data from monthly to quarterly, which CMS said maintains meaningful transparency while reducing administrative burden. It takes effect December 5, 2026, with most data requirements phased in across 2027.

**In plain terms:** Since 2021 hospitals and insurers have had to publish their negotiated prices in machine-readable files. The files turned out to be enormous, duplicated and often wrong, so almost nobody could use them to compare prices. This rule is plumbing: fewer files, cleaner data, penalties for publishing garbage. The catch sits in the same document. Insurers may now refresh some data four times a year instead of twelve, so a price you look up could be three months old.

**Why it matters:** Researchers and employer coalitions have been the main consumers of these files, and quarterly updates make it harder to catch a price change in the quarter it happens.

### A drone sank a Turkish-owned grain ship inside Romania's economic zone, killing two crew

The Royad Mammadov, a Turkish-owned bulk carrier under the flag of Saint Kitts and Nevis, sank in the Black Sea on October 5 after drones struck it about nine nautical miles from Romania's Pescarus platform, inside Romania's exclusive economic zone. Two crew members were killed, eleven were rescued and one was missing. The ship had left the Ukrainian Danube port of Izmail roughly three hours earlier carrying grain, bound for Ravenna, Italy. President Volodymyr Zelensky said two Russian drones hit it in neutral waters and that Russia “consistently chooses terror.” Russia has not claimed the strike.

**In plain terms:** An exclusive economic zone is the water out to 200 nautical miles where a coastal country controls fishing and drilling but not navigation. It is not that country's territory, so a strike there does not trigger NATO's mutual-defense clause the way an attack on Romanian soil would. That gap is the point of where this happened, and none of these facts obliges anyone to respond militarily. Russia has not claimed the attack; the attribution is Ukraine's.

**Why it matters:** Grain leaving Ukraine by sea depends on commercial shipowners and their insurers accepting the risk, and a sinking inside a NATO state's economic zone reprices that risk without anyone having to blockade anything.

**In plain terms:** Medicare Part B covers doctor visits and outpatient care, and most enrollees pay for it out of their Social Security check: $202.90 a month this year. A one-time $90 payment covers a little under half of one month. The Medicare Improvement Fund exists to improve how Medicare operates, not to send cash to enrollees, and by the White House's own account no administration has used it this way before. The payments land four weeks before the midterms.

**Why it matters:** Spending a program-improvement fund on direct payments sets a precedent Congress did not legislate, and the fund was created with $2 billion, so roughly $1.9 billion of it goes out in a single week.

## 9. TECH

### Denmark's civil registry was queried without authorization, exposing records on 8.8 million people

Danish authorities confirmed on October 5 that unauthorized parties ran searches against the CPR, the national civil registration system, exposing names, addresses and CPR personal identification numbers for about 8.8 million people. The registry itself was not breached; the intruders abused a Danish company's legitimate authority to query it. Officials detected irregular activity on October 2 and found the queries had run for roughly ten days in September before anyone noticed. That access has been revoked. The CPR holds about 11 million records, including emigrants and the dead, which is why the figure exceeds Denmark's population of roughly six million. Digitalisation minister Christina Egelund called it a “serious incident.”

**In plain terms:** A CPR number is the single identifier a Dane uses for taxes, banking, healthcare and signing contracts, and it cannot be reissued like a stolen credit card. Nothing was hacked in the usual sense: a company allowed to look people up had that permission used by someone who should not have had it, for ten days before anyone noticed. The weak point was a trusted outside party's access, and a national registry grants that to thousands of them.

**Why it matters:** Every country running a single-identifier digital ID system has the same structure, so the lesson here is that the attack surface is the list of organizations granted lookup rights, not the registry itself.

## 10. TRUMP NEWS

### Trump moved the ad campaign praising him to his super PAC, after about $10 million of it was paid for with federal money

Trump said in a Truth Social post on the night of October 5 that his super PAC, MAGA Inc., will pay for the television advertisements promoting him and his policies, ending the use of taxpayer funds. Federal contracting records show Customs and Border Protection awarded $20 million to the Maryland firm LMD Agency on September 20 for a “National Media Campaign.” AdImpact estimated that about $10.1 million in taxpayer money had already been spent, on spots that ran carrying the disclaimer “Paid for by the U.S. Government.” Trump had earlier called the arrangement “a rather standard thing to do.” Senator Mike Rounds, a Republican, said of the ads, “It's not something that I would've done.” Representative Jamie Raskin said they violated the Hatch Act. Legal experts told the Associated Press the spending appears to conflict with the statutory bar on using appropriated funds for “publicity or propaganda.” The White House has called the ads public service announcements.

**In plain terms:** A super PAC is a private fund and can legally spend unlimited money praising a candidate. Using money Congress gave an agency for the same purpose is different: appropriations law forbids spending federal funds on “publicity or propaganda,” and the Hatch Act restricts federal employees' political activity. These ads were bought with Customs and Border Protection money, then labeled as government-funded. On Monday night the bill moved to a private fund; the ads did not change.

**Why it matters:** Moving future ads to MAGA Inc. does nothing about the roughly $10 million in Customs and Border Protection money already spent, which is the part congressional appropriators control and can demand an accounting for.
