## 1. AI INDUSTRY

### Alphabet's AI build-out pushes its free cash flow below zero while Microsoft's holds up

Alphabet reported third-quarter revenue of $127.6 billion on October 28, up 25 percent, alongside $51.4 billion of capital expenditure and negative free cash flow of $9.07 billion, against the roughly $2.70 billion shortfall analysts expected. Google Cloud's order backlog reached $561 billion, and CFO Anat Ashkenazi said capex will rise significantly in 2027. The stock fell about 10 percent. Microsoft posted revenue of $92.3 billion, up 19 percent, with Azure growth of 48 percent; its shares rose about 3.5 percent.

**In plain terms:** Both companies rent out computing power, and both are spending heavily on the buildings and chips to supply it. Free cash flow is what is left after all of that is paid for, and Alphabet's went below zero: in three months it spent more cash than it took in. Demand is not the problem, since Google Cloud holds $561 billion of signed orders. What investors balked at is how many years of spending come first.

**Why it matters:** The market has stopped reading AI spending as a signal of ambition and started pricing it as a claim on cash that has to be earned back.

## 2. INTERNATIONAL AFFAIRS

### US tells Middle East allies to prepare for possible strikes on Iran after the November 3 midterms

The Associated Press reported on October 28 that US diplomats and defence officials have warned several Middle Eastern governments to prepare for renewed military action against Iran after the November 3 elections, and for Iranian retaliation that could reach energy facilities. Two diplomats briefed on the warnings said a new round could last several days, and stressed that Trump has not made a final decision. The Pentagon moved the carrier USS Theodore Roosevelt and about 9,000 personnel toward the Persian Gulf late last month. The White House declined to comment.

**In plain terms:** The United States appears to be telling friendly governments to prepare for a fight it says it has not yet chosen to start. Iran's likely answer is aimed not at American troops but at oil and gas installations, which is what sets fuel prices worldwide. The timing, pinned to the far side of an election, is the detail to notice. One caveat: this is the Associated Press citing two anonymous diplomats, and other outlets are republishing it rather than confirming it themselves.

**Why it matters:** A decision deferred past an election is still a decision being prepared, and the hardware needed to carry it out is already in position.

## 3. ECONOMICS

### The Fed holds rates steady, and both dissenting votes wanted an increase

The Federal Open Market Committee kept its target range at 3.75 to 4 percent on October 28 in a 10-2 vote, saying inflation remains elevated. Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack dissented, both preferring a quarter-point increase; it was Cook's first dissent since joining the Board in 2022. September's move was a quarter-point rise, so this is a pause inside a tightening cycle. Chair Kevin Warsh called growth strong and inflation too high, and said politics played no role before the midterms. Futures pricing implies a meaningful chance of a December hike.

**In plain terms:** The Fed sets the interest rate that works its way into mortgages, car loans and savings accounts. When officials break ranks over a decision to hold, the usual complaint is that money should be cheaper. Here both dissenters wanted the opposite, because prices are still rising faster than the Fed's 2 percent target. Since the last move was upward, this is a pause in the middle of raising rates, not a pause before cutting them.

**Why it matters:** Holding rates while your only dissenters argue for a hike tells you which direction the next surprise is likely to come from.

## 4. HEALTHCARE

### Lilly's three-target drug beat Ozempic on blood sugar and more than doubled its weight loss

Eli Lilly said on October 28 that retatrutide outperformed Novo Nordisk's Ozempic in TRANSCEND-T2D-2, an open-label Phase 3 trial of 1,298 adults with type 2 diabetes run to 80 weeks. Retatrutide cut A1C by 2.2 percentage points at both doses against 1.8 for Ozempic at its highest approved dose. Weight loss was 44.3 and 47.4 pounds versus 20.9 pounds. Discontinuation over side effects ran at 4.8 and 7.9 percent against 2.7 percent. The weight figures count only patients who stayed on treatment. Lilly plans an FDA submission in early 2027.

**In plain terms:** Ozempic imitates one gut hormone that signals fullness and helps control blood sugar. Retatrutide imitates three at once, which is why it is called a triple-G drug, and hitting three targets produced about twice the weight loss. The catch sits in the dropout numbers: on the higher dose nearly three times as many people quit over side effects. A drug only helps someone who keeps taking it, and the weight results count only those who did.

**Why it matters:** Novo's position in diabetes rested on Ozempic being the benchmark rivals were measured against, and it is now the control arm that lost.

## 5. WILDCARD - AI INDUSTRY

### Anthropic's IPO filing shows $16.3 billion of half-year revenue and an $80.7 billion net loss

Anthropic filed its S-1 publicly on October 26, applying to list on the Nasdaq under the ticker ANT with Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley leading. It reports $16.3 billion of revenue for the first half of 2026 against $1.2 billion a year earlier, and a net loss of $80.7 billion, of which $72.4 billion is a non-cash charge on convertible notes. Compute commitments run as high as $750 billion. The risk factors, about 94 pages, say no insurer or the industry as a whole may have capacity for a catastrophic AI incident. No price range is set.

**In plain terms:** An S-1 is the document a company must file before selling shares to the public, and the law requires it to spell out what could go wrong. Revenue grew more than thirteenfold in a year. The $80.7 billion loss is less alarming than it looks, because most of it is an accounting adjustment on loans that convert into shares rather than cash spent. The notable part is a company stating in a legal filing that its worst case may be uninsurable.

**Why it matters:** Frontier labs have all described these risks in public, but this is the first time one has signed them under securities law, where a misstatement is actionable.

## 6. WILDCARD - INTERNATIONAL AFFAIRS

### Support for the UN resolution against the Cuba embargo falls to its lowest level since 1995

The UN General Assembly adopted its 34th annual resolution calling for an end to the US embargo on Cuba on October 28, by 130 votes to 17 with 25 abstentions. Last year it passed 165 to 7 with 12 abstentions. The no votes included the United States, Israel, Argentina, Hungary and Ukraine; Germany and Italy abstained. US ambassador Mike Waltz told the Assembly that Cuba's crisis is its own government's fault, citing more than $876 million of US exports to Cuba this year. Reporting attributes the shift to an intensified US lobbying campaign.

**In plain terms:** Since 1992 the General Assembly has voted annually to tell the United States to drop its trade ban on Cuba, and almost every country votes yes. The vote changes nothing in law, because the Assembly cannot order Washington to do anything. Its only real function is as a scoreboard of who will side with the United States when doing so costs little. Thirty-five countries came off the yes column in one year, and that margin is the news.

**Why it matters:** The lobbying machinery that moved 35 votes on a symbolic resolution is the same machinery available for votes that are not symbolic.

## 7. WILDCARD - HEALTHCARE

### HHS says it will not enforce against pharmacies compounding six unapproved peptides

The Department of Health and Human Services said on October 28 it will use enforcement discretion not to act against state-licensed pharmacies, federal facilities and licensed physicians compounding six peptides: BPC-157, epitalon, KPV, MOTS-c, semax and TB-500. None is FDA-approved, and the agency's own scientists concluded there is insufficient data to establish that they are safe and effective. An FDA advisory committee backed the six in July; STAT reported that several panel members had ties to peptide sellers, which HHS said cleared standard ethics checks. HHS said it may reconsider if safety problems emerge.

**In plain terms:** Compounding is when a pharmacy mixes a drug to order for one patient instead of buying it ready-made. Normally the FDA clears a drug only after trials show it is safe and works. Nothing has been cleared here. The health department has only said it will not punish pharmacies for making these six substances, which is a different thing and can be withdrawn at any time. They already sold widely in the fitness market as research chemicals.

**Why it matters:** Enforcement discretion is the route by which a substance reaches patients at scale without ever meeting the standard it was supposed to meet.

## 8. WILDCARD - AI INDUSTRY

### SK hynix books the first 100-trillion-won quarter in its history at an operating margin near 80 percent

SK hynix reported third-quarter revenue of 104.6 trillion won on October 28, up 32 percent from the prior quarter and 328 percent from a year earlier, its first quarter above 100 trillion won. Operating profit was about 83.3 trillion won, a company record and a margin near 80 percent, against consensus closer to 77 trillion. The company began mass production and shipment of HBM4 memory in the quarter and said long-term supply agreements give it revenue visibility through 2028. It outlined roughly $75.4 billion of shareholder returns for 2026.

**In plain terms:** Every AI chip needs memory beside it to feed it data, and SK hynix makes the fastest kind, called HBM. An operating margin near 80 percent means roughly 80 cents of every sales dollar is profit before tax. You almost never see that in manufacturing, because it happens only when customers have nowhere else to buy. Revenue more than quadrupled in a year, and the contracts running to 2028 lock in boom-era prices in advance.

**Why it matters:** Margins that wide measure scarcity rather than skill, and the multi-year contracts book that scarcity into revenue through 2028 regardless of what demand does next.

## 9. TECH

### South Korea fines Google and Apple 63.7 billion won in the first enforcement of its in-app payment law

The Korea Media and Communications Commission fined Google 42.9 billion won and Apple 20.81 billion won on October 28, about 63.7 billion won combined or roughly $45 million to $48 million, for requiring developers to use the companies' own payment systems and for unfairly delaying app reviews. It is the first enforcement under the 2021 amendment to the Telecommunications Business Act, which bars app stores from mandating a payment method. Both must allow links to external payment pages and revise commission structures within three months. Apple said it disagrees and is reviewing options; Google said it was disappointed.

**In plain terms:** When you buy something inside a phone app, Google and Apple have long required the payment to run through their own systems, on which they take a cut of up to 30 percent. South Korea banned that in 2021, the first country anywhere to do so, and has now, five years later, actually imposed a penalty. The money is trivial at this scale. The order with real force is the three-month deadline to let developers link out.

**Why it matters:** The European Union and others wrote comparable rules, so Korea's remedy rather than its fine is the template other regulators will copy.

Sources: [The Korea Times](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/business/companies/20261028/korea-fines-google-apple-combined-476-mil-over-in-app-payment-practices) · [Korea JoongAng Daily](https://www.koreajoongangdaily.com/business/korea-fines-google-and-apple-476-million-over-inapp-payments/12943573) · [UPI](https://www.upi.com/Top_News/World-News/2026/10/28/antitrust-google-apple-in-app-payments-app-stores/9711793232887/)

## 10. TRUMP NEWS

### Three Wall Street firms are paying Trump's company for his posts 50 milliseconds early

Reuters reported on October 28 that Bloomberg, Susquehanna International Group and Jump Trading have signed up for Truth API, a Trump Media product delivering posts from ten accounts, Trump's among them, roughly 50 milliseconds ahead of the ordinary app for up to $100,000 a month. Trump Media called it an industry-standard API product and said the reporting rested on misleading information; Reuters said it could not confirm individual payments. Trump Media's SEC filings list Donald Trump Jr. as trustee of the family trust holding the president's stake, with sole voting and investment power. No agency has made a finding and no charges have been filed.

**In plain terms:** The president's posts move markets, because a sentence about tariffs or a company can swing prices within seconds. A company in which his family trust holds a large stake sells a feed delivering those posts a twentieth of a second before everyone else, and high-speed trading firms pay up to $100,000 a month for it. Fifty milliseconds is ample time for a computer to trade. Nothing has been ruled illegal; two senators have asked the SEC to look.

**Why it matters:** The arrangement turns presidential speech into a timed, priced commodity sold by a company the president's family controls, a structure no existing ethics rule was drafted to address.
