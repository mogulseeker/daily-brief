## AI INDUSTRY

### Marvell agrees to buy Lumentum for $115 billion, the largest semiconductor deal on record

Marvell Technology said Monday it will acquire Lumentum, which makes lasers and optical transceivers, in an all-stock deal valuing it at $115 billion including debt, or about $1,132 a share, a 23.9 percent premium to Lumentum's October 28 close. Lumentum holders get 4.5 Marvell shares each and will own about 33 percent of the combined company. Both boards approved unanimously. Marvell's previous largest purchase was Inphi, at $10 billion in 2021.

**In plain terms:** Lumentum makes the parts that turn electrical signals into pulses of light so data can travel down fiber. An AI data center is tens of thousands of chips that must talk constantly, and copper runs out of speed over distance. Marvell designs the chips at either end of those links, so buying Lumentum means owning the whole path. Note the premium: Marvell is paying up in its own shares just as investors started punishing AI spending.

**Why it matters:** The money in AI infrastructure is shifting from the chips themselves to the plumbing that connects them, and this deal prices that plumbing at a level the chip industry has never paid for anything.

## INTERNATIONAL AFFAIRS

### Iranian negotiators leave Doha with a seven-day draft circulating, as two more tankers hit mines in Hormuz

Iranian negotiators left talks with Qatari mediators in Doha on Sunday. An official told Reuters the departure was not over terms and that a draft is circulating setting a seven-day timeline of daily confidence-building steps. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said the Strait of Hormuz could reopen within two days under a deal. A senior Iranian official separately denied any new Qatari proposal, calling its ideas "unacceptable." The same day the Revolutionary Guard navy said two tankers hit mines. Brent crude fell 4.3 percent Monday to about $108.68.

**In plain terms:** The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow passage at the mouth of the Gulf that about a fifth of the world's oil normally moves through. Iran has mined it and the US Navy is blockading Iran, so each side holds something the other wants back. One Iranian official describes a draft under negotiation and another says Qatar brought nothing acceptable. Oil fell anyway, so traders are betting on the first account.

**Why it matters:** Tehran's claim that it can reopen the strait in two days is itself the bargaining chip, because it concedes that the closure is a tap Iran controls rather than damage that takes months to repair.

## ECONOMICS

### South Korea's October exports nearly doubled to a record $115.3 billion on AI chip demand

South Korea's trade ministry reported Sunday that October exports rose 93.7 percent from a year earlier to a record $115.3 billion, just ahead of the 93.2 percent median forecast in a Reuters poll. Semiconductor shipments jumped 259.4 percent to $56.44 billion and computer exports rose 496.6 percent on AI server demand. Exports for January through October totalled $930 billion, and the trade minister said $1 trillion for the year will be passed in November.

**In plain terms:** South Korea is where much of the world's memory is made, by Samsung and SK Hynix. Its export report lands within days of the month ending, making it one of the first hard readings on global demand rather than a survey of opinions. It says AI data-center construction is still accelerating, not cooling. The uncomfortable part is concentration: chips were close to half of what the country sold abroad in October.

**Why it matters:** This is the cleanest hard data confirming that AI capital spending is still rising, which is awkward timing for equity investors who spent last week selling the companies doing the spending.

## HEALTHCARE

### ACA open enrollment opens for 2027 with a second straight year of double-digit rate increases

Open enrollment for 2027 Affordable Care Act coverage began Sunday in most states, running through January 15 on HealthCare.gov. KFF's review of filings from 276 insurers in every state and Washington, DC found a median proposed increase of 15 percent, with 63 percent of filings between 10 and 25 percent; finalized benchmark silver premiums rose 13 percent on average. The enhanced premium tax credits expired at the start of 2026. The House passed a three-year extension in January and the Senate has not acted.

**In plain terms:** ACA plans are the insurance you buy yourself if no employer covers you and you are too young for Medicare. Extra federal subsidies used to cap what most buyers paid, and those lapsed this year. So two things push the bill up at once: the sticker price is rising about 15 percent, and the discount on top of it is gone. Insurers say part of it is circular. When prices rose, healthier people dropped out, leaving a sicker pool.

**Why it matters:** The subsidy lapse and the rate increase land in the same enrollment window that closes just after the midterms, which means the political bill for it arrives after the votes are counted.

## WILDCARD - AI INDUSTRY

### OpenAI cuts its funding round to $20 billion and signs four Abu Dhabi deals

OpenAI has cut the target for its current round to up to $20 billion from roughly $30 billion, the Financial Times reported, at a $1.25 trillion valuation. UAE investors including MGX are leading alongside Coatue; the Wall Street Journal reported about $11 billion secured and a tepid response from some large investors. OpenAI's March round closed at $852 billion. On Monday Sam Altman signed four agreements in Abu Dhabi covering ADNOC's operations, a "Machine-Readable Government" programme piloted on food imports, Mubadala investment analysis, and a five-year Inception42 deal.

**In plain terms:** A funding target is what a company hopes to raise. Cutting it by a third while holding the price per share means fewer buyers at that price, not a cheaper company. OpenAI has responded by leaning on investors who will still write large cheques, which are Gulf sovereign funds. The Abu Dhabi deals are the other half of that: the emirate supplies capital and electricity, and gets OpenAI's models inside its oil company and its government.

**Why it matters:** Pairing a shrinking round with state deals in the same week shows where frontier AI money is actually available now, and it is not from the institutional investors who funded the last three years.

### Wall Street closed October with its worst day since June as AI spending fears hit tech

The Nasdaq Composite fell 2.86 percent on Friday to 25,437.00, its largest one-day drop since June 5, while the S&P 500 lost 1.82 percent and the Dow 1.09 percent. Intel fell about 29 percent on weak guidance. Nvidia's 4.2 percent drop was the heaviest single drag on the S&P 500, and Amazon disclosed negative free cash flow of $42.4 billion over the prior twelve months. **In plain terms:** Free cash flow is what a business has left after paying for the buildings and equipment it bought. For three years investors treated AI spending as a reason to buy these shares. Last week they treated the same spending as a cost. What changed was which column investors filed it under.

**Why it matters:** The selling hit the firms spending on AI rather than the ones selling to them, which is the pattern you would expect if the market is starting to price the buildout as a liability rather than a growth story.

## WILDCARD - INTERNATIONAL AFFAIRS

### The UN's Western Sahara peacekeeping mission ends after 35 years

MINURSO, created by Security Council resolution 690 in 1991, began winding down on November 1 after its mandate lapsed at midnight on October 31 with no renewal. Russia vetoed a US-drafted resolution that would have endorsed Morocco's autonomy plan while shrinking the force, and a competing Russian resolution was defeated. Secretary-General Antonio Guterres said the UN "stands ready" to keep supporting peace efforts. Morocco's royal palace called the non-renewal a "significant development"; the Polisario Front said it was concerned by the deadlock.

**In plain terms:** Western Sahara is a stretch of desert coast that Morocco controls and that the Polisario Front says should be an independent country. The UN sent peacekeepers in 1991 to watch a ceasefire and run a referendum on which it would be. That vote never happened, because the two sides could never agree on who was eligible to take part. The mission is ending not because the dispute was settled but because the Council could not agree on wording.

**Why it matters:** The ceasefire monitors are leaving a dispute that is not resolved, which removes the mechanism that has kept a frozen conflict frozen since 1991.

## WILDCARD - HEALTHCARE

### Labor Department finalizes rule making drug middlemen disclose their fees to employers

The Labor Department's Employee Benefits Security Administration finalized its pharmacy benefit manager transparency rule on October 29. PBMs and affiliated brokers must disclose compensation, including manufacturer rebates, spread pricing, money recouped from pharmacies and payments for formulary placement, to fiduciaries of self-insured ERISA plans with 100 or more participants. Disclosures are due at contract renewal and every six months, with an annual audit right. The rule takes effect in July 2027. The department estimates savings of up to $22 billion over ten years. PBMs called it government overreach.

**In plain terms:** A pharmacy benefit manager is the company your employer hires to run its drug coverage: it picks which medicines are covered and negotiates prices. It is paid partly out of those deals, through drugmaker rebates and by keeping the gap between what it bills the employer and pays the pharmacy. Until now the employer paying the bill often could not see those amounts. The rule caps nothing; it only requires that the numbers be shown.

**Why it matters:** Disclosure rules change markets through contract renegotiation rather than enforcement, so the effect will show up in what employers pay in 2028, not in any penalty.

## TECH

### Fake nuclear-attack alerts were pushed through both Ukraine's and Russia's official emergency apps overnight

Ukraine's public Air Alert app began issuing false warnings shortly before 11pm Sunday, one claiming Russia had launched a hypersonic Oreshnik missile and another declaring a nuclear alert. Ukraine's air force said the system appeared to have been hacked by Russia, noting the messages were in Russian; the app was suspended while sirens kept working. Russia's Emergencies Ministry then reported a "massive cyberattack" causing "the spread of false information to the public," including a claim that warheads had been launched across all Russian regions. The pro-Ukrainian group Atesh claimed the Russian breach, unverified.

**In plain terms:** Both countries run phone apps that push official air-raid warnings, and in a war people act on them within seconds. That trust is what makes the app a target: you do not need to launch a missile to cause panic if you can make the government's own system announce one. Neither breach caused mass panic, and Ukraine's sirens kept working. Some Ukrainians said afterwards they now trust the official alerts less, a cost that outlives the fix.

**Why it matters:** An alert system only works if people believe it, so the attack degrades a wartime capability without touching any weapon, and the damage persists after the breach is closed.

## TRUMP NEWS

### Judge throws out the Justice Department's bid to delay Alaska's Senate certification; Trump calls the suit "most meritful"

US District Judge Sharon Gleason dismissed the Justice Department's Alaska lawsuit on Sunday. It had asked the court to segregate ballots and delay certification of the Senate race if Republican incumbent Dan Sullivan lost, because a second candidate named Daniel J. Sullivan was on the ballot. Gleason held the department lacked standing, writing that it "failed to satisfy its burden to show that its alleged injury is redressable by a court order." Trump, asked about the ruling on the tarmac in Iowa, said he had not heard about it and called the suit "most meritful" and "a very important lawsuit." It was the third such loss in four days: on October 30 the Ninth Circuit again refused, 2-1, to make California set aside about 20,000 ballots, and Judge Trina Thompson barred the department and DHS from demanding that any state segregate ballots or purge voters.

**In plain terms:** Standing is the rule that you must show a court a specific injury it can actually fix before it will hear you. Gleason did not rule on whether the two Sullivans confused anyone. She ruled the government had not shown a remedy a judge could order, partly because finding out which Sullivan a voter meant would mean opening secret ballots. Trump called the suit highly meritorious in the same remarks in which he said he had not heard it was dismissed.

**Why it matters:** Courts have now rejected the ballot-segregation theory in Alaska, California and nationwide within four days, which means the legal groundwork for contesting Tuesday's results was tested in advance and failed.
