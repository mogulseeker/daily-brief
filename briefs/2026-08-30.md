## 1. AI INDUSTRY

### OpenAI will stop supplying its models to Cursor now that SpaceX owns it

OpenAI told SpaceX on August 28 that it is winding down the contract that lets the AI coding tool Cursor call OpenAI models, with access ending November 12. SpaceX agreed in June to buy Cursor's maker, Anysphere, for $60 billion in stock and closed the purchase on August 14; OpenAI's contract allowed it to exit within a set window after a change of ownership. OpenAI said it "cannot be confident that SpaceX will use our technology within our terms of service, based on our experience with Elon Musk's companies violating contracts." Cursor chief executive Michael Truell said OpenAI models handle about 5% of Cursor's traffic and that developers can still reach them using their own OpenAI keys.

**In plain terms:** Cursor is a widely used program that helps software developers write code, and it works by passing requests to whichever AI model the developer picks, from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google or xAI. Elon Musk's rocket company bought Cursor's maker two weeks ago, and OpenAI's contract let it walk away if the owner changed. The number worth noticing is 5%: OpenAI is cutting off a customer that barely used it, so the loss lands mostly on OpenAI's revenue, not on Cursor's users.

**Why it matters:** Access to frontier models is now something labs will withdraw from a rival's subsidiary, which makes "which models does this tool support" a question about who owns the tool rather than what it can do.

## 2. INTERNATIONAL AFFAIRS

### A Russian strike on an ammunition warehouse near Kyiv kills 37, and Ukraine opens a case against its own officials

A Russian drone hit a warehouse in the village of Myla, in the Bucha district west of Kyiv, late Friday. Ukrainian officials said the building held shells, mines and other ammunition; the blasts and fires that followed damaged at least 50 residential buildings and killed 37 people, making it the deadliest single attack of the war this year, above the 31 killed in Kyiv on July 2. Russia's Defence Ministry said it struck a store of components and launch boosters for long-range drones. President Volodymyr Zelenskyy called the deaths the result of "terrible negligence on the part of those who stored explosives right next to people," noted that Ukrainian law forbids it, and said criminal proceedings had been opened against officials at state firms including the arms maker Ukroboronprom.

**In plain terms:** Bucha is a commuter district on the western edge of Kyiv, Ukraine's capital, full of ordinary houses. Most of the 37 deaths were not caused by the drone itself but by what it landed on: a store of shells and mines that exploded and set the neighbourhood on fire. Ukraine's own president is pointing at his own side, because Ukrainian law says ammunition cannot be kept beside homes and it was. That is why prosecutors are investigating state arms officials rather than only counting a Russian strike.

**Why it matters:** Ukraine's wartime logistics have been pushed into civilian neighbourhoods, and the cost of that shortcut is now measured in a death toll larger than the strike itself would have produced.

## 3. ECONOMICS

### Iran's trade shrinks by a third and its supreme leader orders the dollar phased out

President Masoud Pezeshkian said Iranian imports and exports have fallen between 25% and 35%, with imports down more. Supreme leader Ayatollah Mojtaba Khamenei called for "gradually phasing out the US dollar from playing a pivotal role" in Iranian transactions and making a self-sufficient "Resistance Economy" the country's central focus. The pressure is a naval blockade that Washington reimposed on July 14 after Iranian attacks on tankers in the Strait of Hormuz; US Central Command said that as of August 28 it had redirected 82 commercial vessels, disabled three and boarded two. Sources conflict on how far exports have fallen: Kpler tanker data cited by CNBC puts August crude loadings near 260,000 barrels a day, more than 80% below August 2025, while The National reports Iranian oil exports have fallen to zero. The National also reports inflation above 80% and the rial at record lows.

**In plain terms:** Almost all oil is bought and sold in US dollars, which means Washington can see and block a large share of Iran's trade without ever touching a ship. Iran is now being squeezed both ways: a US naval blockade stops the tankers, and dollar clearing stops the payments. Phasing out the dollar is a plan to make the second squeeze stop working, but it takes years and willing trading partners. The export numbers do not agree between outlets, so treat "roughly collapsed" as the safe reading rather than any single figure.

**Why it matters:** A country of about 90 million losing a third of its trade is a supply and migration shock that shows up in its neighbours long before it shows up in any negotiation.

## 4. HEALTHCARE

### In 14 states, one more rural maternity closure would put Medicaid patients an hour from a delivery room

A KFF analysis released this week measured the drive from every rural hospital that delivers babies for Medicaid patients to the next in-state hospital that does. The median gap is 43 minutes for rural hospitals against 13 minutes for urban ones, and for one in four rural hospitals the nearest alternative is already at least an hour away. In 14 states, including Alaska, Nevada, North Dakota, Montana, Wyoming, New Mexico, Arizona, Colorado, Florida, Hawaii, Massachusetts, New Hampshire, Vermont and Virginia, losing the closest rural hospital or its obstetric unit would push the median trip past an hour. The analysis lands as roughly $1 trillion in federal Medicaid funding is cut over the next decade, including about $137 billion from rural areas.

**In plain terms:** Medicaid is the government insurance programme for people with low incomes, and it pays for a large share of births in rural America. Maternity wards are among the first things a struggling rural hospital shuts, because they need staff on call around the clock for a small number of deliveries. This analysis measures what happens after that: not whether care exists, but how far away the next one is. An hour matters in obstetrics specifically, because bleeding and other emergencies in labour are counted in minutes.

**Why it matters:** The funding cuts and the closures are being debated as budget lines, but the thing that actually changes for patients is drive time, and that is the number nobody votes on.

Sources: [KFF](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/availability-of-rural-hospitals-providing-inpatient-maternity-care-in-medicaid/) · [Stateline](https://stateline.org/2026/08/28/more-rural-hospital-closures-would-mean-long-drives-for-pregnant-women-seeking-obstetric-care/)

## 5. WILDCARD - INTERNATIONAL AFFAIRS

### Nepal's flood death toll passes 700 as searches continue for 2,400 missing

Four days after a wall of water, ice and debris tore down the Trishuli valley on the Nepal-Tibet border, Nepali authorities put the confirmed dead at 626 on Saturday, with more than 700 dead across Nepal and at least 16 killed on the Chinese side. More than 2,400 people remain missing in Nepal, including about 90 Americans, and more than 3,700 have been rescued. Authorities believe over 100 people are trapped in a mud-filled tunnel at a hydropower construction site in Rasuwa district. The State Department said Saturday that US humanitarian aid had been raised to $3.6 million, up from the $500,000 announced two days earlier.

**In plain terms:** The flood is thought to have started when a glacier near Langtang Lirung collapsed, which released a huge volume of trapped meltwater at once; seismometers around the world recorded the collapse as a magnitude 5.2 tremor. That is why the wave arrived with almost no warning and why the missing count is so much larger than the confirmed dead. Many of the missing are foreigners who were in the valley to trek, work or make a pilgrimage. The gap between 626 confirmed and 2,400 missing is not two separate groups; most of the missing are likely dead.

**Why it matters:** Glacier-collapse floods give no lead time at all, which makes them a category that early-warning systems built for monsoon rain do not catch.

## 6. WILDCARD - AI INDUSTRY

### A judge rules the Pentagon's blacklisting of Anthropic was retaliation, and orders it undone

US District Judge Rita Lin of the Northern District of California ruled on Thursday, in a 59-page order, that the Defense Department violated the First Amendment and the Fifth Amendment's due process clause when it designated Anthropic a supply chain risk, and ordered the government to rescind every directive issued against the company. Lin wrote that the designation was made "based on a desire to make a public example" of Anthropic, and that "the empty invocation of national security is not a blank check to punish and retaliate against government critics." The dispute grew out of a $200 million contract over deploying Claude models on classified systems, in which Anthropic sought contractual limits barring use in autonomous lethal weapons or domestic mass surveillance and the department refused. It was the first time a US company had been publicly designated a supply chain risk under the statute, which was written to guard military systems against foreign sabotage.

**In plain terms:** A "supply chain risk" designation is a procurement tool meant for companies that might be compromised by a hostile foreign government; being on the list makes it very hard to sell anything to the federal government. The Pentagon applied it to a US company after a contract argument about whether its AI could be used for autonomous weapons and mass surveillance. The judge's ruling is not that the Pentagon must buy from Anthropic. It is that punishing a contractor for the position it took publicly is retaliation, and that calling something national security does not exempt it from review.

**Why it matters:** It sets a limit on using procurement blacklists as a punishment, which is the lever the government has been reaching for most often with AI companies that refuse a use case.

### Masked settlers attack a Palestinian woman and an NBC News crew in the West Bank

Four NBC News journalists were interviewing Aida Ahmed Abdullah and her family in the Palestinian village of Jalud, near Nablus, on Saturday when a car with Israeli plates stopped and at least four masked men in dark clothing got out, hit Abdullah and the crew with sticks and threw rocks at them. Abdullah and three crew members were injured, and camera equipment and a vehicle were damaged. Separate footage from the nearby village of Qusra showed settlers attacking a Palestinian home while Israeli soldiers stood by. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu condemned "the criminal acts of violence committed in the villages of Kosra and Jalud by a handful of rioters who violate the law, cause enormous injustice to the law-abiding settler public, hinder the IDF in fulfilling its missions and harm Israel's standing in the world."

**In plain terms:** The West Bank is Palestinian territory that Israel has occupied since 1967, where Israeli settlers live in communities among Palestinian villages. Attacks by settlers on Palestinians are frequent and rarely filmed by outsiders; what is different here is that a foreign news crew was standing there with cameras when it happened, and that soldiers were recorded nearby without intervening in the second village. Netanyahu's statement condemns the attackers, and the wording is precise about who he is defending: he frames them as a handful of rioters who damage the settler public's reputation.

**Why it matters:** Video shot by an international outlet, rather than by residents, is much harder to dispute, which is why this incident drew a prime ministerial statement when similar ones do not.

## 8. WILDCARD - INTERNATIONAL AFFAIRS

### Sudanese protesters shut the Port Sudan highway over 20-hour blackouts in a heatwave

Protesters in Sudan's Red Sea State blocked the main highway between Port Sudan and Khartoum on Friday and kept it closed through Saturday, with the blockade extending across the Suakin and Gebeit areas. Demonstrators in Suakin described blackouts of up to 12 hours a day; in Port Sudan itself outages have run more than 20 hours a day alongside a severe water shortage, as temperatures climb along the Red Sea coast. A demonstrator in Gebeit told Sudan Tribune the blockade was a last resort after repeated appeals to authorities went nowhere. Port Sudan has served as the country's administrative and logistical hub since government institutions relocated from Khartoum during the war.

**In plain terms:** Sudan has been fighting a civil war since 2023, and when the fighting made Khartoum unusable the government moved its offices to Port Sudan on the Red Sea coast. That city is now where aid, imports and the state itself pass through. Cutting the road between it and Khartoum is the most disruptive thing residents can do without weapons, which is a measure of how little else has worked. Twenty hours of blackout a day in Red Sea heat is not an inconvenience; it means no refrigeration, no water pumping and no functioning clinics.

**Why it matters:** The war has been described in terms of who holds which city, but the state is now being tested by whether it can keep the lights on in the one city it fully controls.

## 9. TECHNOLOGY

### China's largest memory-chip maker sues the Pentagon to get off its military-ties list

ChangXin Memory Technologies, China's biggest maker of DRAM chips, filed suit in federal court in Washington on Friday against the Defense Department, naming Secretary Pete Hegseth as a defendant, seeking removal from the Section 1260H list of companies the US says support China's military. "CXMT is not affiliated with the Chinese military," the complaint says. "CXMT designs, produces, and sells DRAM chips for civilian and commercial use, not for military use." The company says it spent more than a year supplying information to the Pentagon, that the department published a notice in February indicating it would be delisted and withdrew it the same day, and that it was kept on the list in June. Under a separate provision, federal agencies are barred from using goods containing CXMT semiconductors starting in December 2027. CXMT revenue rose 874% in the first half of 2026 amid an AI-driven memory shortage.

**In plain terms:** DRAM is the fast short-term memory that sits next to a processor; AI servers need enormous amounts of it, and Samsung, SK hynix and Micron cannot make enough. That shortage is why a Chinese memory maker's revenue can grow almost ninefold in six months. The Pentagon's list does not ban selling chips to the US outright, but it marks a company as military-linked, which scares off customers and eventually locks it out of anything the federal government touches. CXMT's core argument is procedural: it says the Pentagon nearly delisted it, reversed course the same day, and never explained why.

**Why it matters:** The world is short of exactly the chips this company makes, so the case tests whether an export-control designation survives contact with a supply shortage.

Sources: [Reuters](https://srnnews.com/cxmt-sues-pentagon-over-inclusion-on-list-of-companies-tied-to-chinas-military/) · [South China Morning Post](https://www.scmp.com/tech/big-tech/article/3365751/cxmt-joins-growing-list-chinese-tech-firms-suing-us-pentagon-over-blacklists) · [Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/world-news/chipmaker-cxmt-sues-pentagon-over-chinese-military-company-designation-126082900100_1.html)

The New York Times reported Thursday, in a story bylined by Maggie Haberman and three colleagues and sourced to four people familiar with the planning, that Trump will skip the 25th-anniversary ceremony at the National September 11 Memorial in Manhattan because he was told he could not deliver a speech there, and will appear at a Pentagon ceremony instead as a featured speaker. **In plain terms:** The ceremony at the site of the World Trade Center has a standing rule, in place since 2012, that politicians may attend and read names but not give speeches. Trump denies the reported reason, and the Times says its sources are solid, so that specific point remains contested. What is not contested is the post itself, because he wrote it: the insults, the demand that a reporter surrender her earnings, and the threat to sue the body that awarded her a prize are all his own words.

**Why it matters:** The threat is aimed at a prize committee rather than at the reporting, which is a suit over a judgment about journalism rather than over any disputed fact.
