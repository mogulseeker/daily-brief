## 1. AI INDUSTRY

### Texas sues OpenAI and Sam Altman, seeking court-ordered safety review before new models ship

Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton filed a 67-page suit on Friday in Collin County against OpenAI and chief executive Sam Altman under the state's Deceptive Trade Practices Act and its Securing Children Online through Parental Empowerment Act. Texas says OpenAI marketed ChatGPT on safety and accuracy claims it knew were false and skipped required age verification. It asks for $10,000 per violation, an order keeping minors off the product, and sign-off by an independent safety evaluator before new model releases. OpenAI said governments have an important role in setting robust safety standards.

**In plain terms:** A state attorney general is a state's top lawyer, and Texas argues OpenAI sold ChatGPT with safety promises it could not keep. Two demands go past money: one would block under-18s in Texas, the other would put an outside examiner between OpenAI and the public, so no new model ships until someone outside the company approves it. These are accusations, not findings; no judge has ruled.

**Why it matters:** A court-appointed gatekeeper on releases would be the first time an outside party held a veto over when a frontier AI system ships, a far larger constraint than any fine.

**In plain terms:** A hospital system's operating margin is what is left from treating patients after the cost of treating them. Mass General Brigham kept 0.3%, about 30 cents on every hundred dollars, leaving almost nothing for equipment or buildings. Last year's cuts hit back-office staff; this round reaches doctors, which is the signal. Management said in writing that trimming administration would not close the gap.

**Why it matters:** When one of the best-funded academic systems in the country cuts clinicians to reach a 1.5% margin, the squeeze is structural rather than local, and smaller hospitals have far less cushion.

## 7. WILDCARD, HEALTHCARE

### CMS and the states put $275 million into recruiting nurses for rural nursing homes

The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services announced an initiative called Nurses for Nursing Homes on Friday, offering nurses and nursing students up to $40,000 in student loan repayment, a $10,000 stipend, or both, for three years of work at a qualifying nursing home. All rural facilities qualify, plus others in severe shortage areas. CMS committed $100 million and states at least $175 million, drawn from civil monetary penalties that CMS and states collect from nursing homes. It is expected to support more than 4,000 nurses and opens October 26. Trump announced it at a rally in Fort Myers, Florida.

**In plain terms:** A nursing home needs licensed nurses on shift to admit residents at all, and rural homes lose candidates to hospitals that pay more. This offers to clear up to $40,000 of a nurse's student debt for three years of rural service. The detail worth noting is the funding: fines levied on nursing homes for violations are being recycled into staffing those same facilities. Across 4,000 nurses, it is a targeted fix, not a cure for the shortage.

**Why it matters:** Funding staffing from penalty revenue ties the program's size to how much nursing homes are fined, making it a workaround for a workforce gap Congress has not appropriated money to close.

## 8. WILDCARD, HEALTHCARE

### House Oversight asks whether the three largest pharmacy middlemen audited pharmacies in retaliation

House Oversight Chairman James Comer sent letters Friday to CVS Caremark, Express Scripts and Optum Rx asking whether they used punitive audits against independent pharmacies that publicly backed H.R. 6610, the Pharmacists Fight Back in FEHB Plans Act. The committee says audit notices spiked at supporting pharmacies after the bill's July markup, and cites one audit over a medication priced at $0.99 that consumed hundreds of staff hours. Comer requested audit totals for independent pharmacies in every state, split between pharmacies the companies own and those they do not. Briefings are set for October 30, documents for November 6. No findings have been made.

**In plain terms:** A pharmacy benefit manager sits between drug makers, insurers and pharmacies and decides what a pharmacy gets paid. The three named here handle most US prescriptions and also own pharmacies competing with the independents they pay. An audit means demanding paperwork proving a claim was legitimate, which costs a small pharmacy real staff time. The committee's claim is about timing: notices rose after pharmacies backed a bill these companies oppose.

**Why it matters:** Audit rates split by ownership would be the first comparable dataset on whether these companies treat their own stores differently, the factual basis PBM legislation has lacked.

## 9. TECH

### SK Hynix pulls Solidigm's US listing after South Korean regulators said it could breach dual-listing rules

SK Hynix said in a regulatory filing on Friday that it had halted its review of pre-IPO and IPO options for Solidigm, its US flash-memory unit, citing recent capital market conditions. South Korea's Financial Services Commission and Financial Supervisory Service had said a US listing could violate domestic rules restricting dual listings, in which a subsidiary lists separately from its listed parent. SK Hynix had weighed a raise reported at $10 billion to $15 billion, which would have been the largest US semiconductor IPO on record. Shares reversed early losses to close 4.4% higher. Reported deal size and the share move vary across outlets.

**In plain terms:** SK Hynix is a South Korean memory-chip maker, and Solidigm is the storage business it bought from Intel. Listing Solidigm separately in New York would have raised cash, but South Korean rules restrict carving a subsidiary out of a listed parent, because existing shareholders already paid for that business and can watch its value move to new owners. The filing blames market conditions; the regulator's objection came first. Investors treated the cancellation as good news.

**Why it matters:** A regulator blocking the largest planned chip listing on governance grounds shows where AI-era capital can be raised is now set by domestic shareholder-protection rules, not only investor appetite.

**In plain terms:** Certification is the routine step where a state declares who won. The agency being sued is the one that tried to remove the confusing candidate and was overruled by Alaska's own Supreme Court. The complaint rests on a law's preamble, the opening passage stating purpose rather than imposing any requirement.

**Why it matters:** A filing that asks a court to withhold certification on only one outcome, against the election office that already tried to prevent the confusion, makes the federal government a party to a count it has not yet seen.
