AI Industry

Nvidia will guarantee up to $105 billion of lease payments for one OpenAI data center

OpenAI announced Monday a data center campus in Pike County, Ohio, built, owned and operated by SoftBank-backed SB Energy under a 20-year lease, with Nvidia guaranteeing as much as $105 billion of OpenAI's lease obligations. Nvidia is also investing $1.5 billion in SB Energy and securing up to 8 gigawatts of AI computing capacity at the site. Initial capacity is 4.25 GW, supported by 10 GW of new power generation, coming online in phases from 2028 on private land and former federal uranium-enrichment property. The developers project 35,000 construction jobs and 2,500 permanent positions.

In plain termsNvidia makes the chips that AI runs on. Here it is not writing a cheque - it is promising the landlord that if OpenAI cannot pay rent on a building full of Nvidia chips, Nvidia will. That promise is what lets someone else borrow the money to build the thing. The catch is circularity: Nvidia is underwriting its own customer's ability to keep buying Nvidia chips, so demand looks sturdier than an arm's-length market would produce. A gigawatt is roughly one large nuclear reactor's output, and this site is planned around ten of them.

Why it mattersThe AI buildout has outgrown what its customers can fund from cash flow, and the chipmaker is now absorbing its buyers' credit risk to keep orders moving.

International Affairs

The US-Iran negotiating deadline expired with no deal, and both sides escalated

The 60-day window set by the June US-Iran memorandum of understanding to reach a final deal expired Monday with no agreement. Trump said he will not extend it and will apply economic pressure instead, telling reporters Iran is unlikely to accept his terms and calling for its surrender. He also threatened to "bomb the shit out of" Oman if it obstructs US efforts to reopen the Strait of Hormuz, and called making the strait US territory "a great idea." A senior Iranian official told Reuters that Tehran will shift to a "fully offensive" military posture and would conduct a "timely and precise" strike to break the US naval blockade if diplomacy fails.

In plain termsThe Strait of Hormuz is a narrow stretch of sea that about a fifth of the world's oil travels through, and the US and Iran have been fighting over control of it. In June the two sides signed a document giving themselves 60 days to reach peace. That clock ran out Monday with nothing agreed, and each side answered by escalating rather than extending. Iran's threat came from one unnamed official speaking to a single news agency, so treat the exact wording as reported, not confirmed.

Why it mattersWith the negotiating deadline gone, the ceiling on oil prices is now set by shipping risk rather than by diplomacy, which is why bond and energy markets moved before a shot was fired.

Sources: The Irish Times (Reuters) · CBS News · CNBC

Economics

A global bond selloff pushed the US 10-year yield to a 19-month high

The US 10-year Treasury yield rose about two basis points to 4.75% on Tuesday, its highest in 19 months and the highest since early 2025, as a worldwide government-bond selloff deepened. The 30-year yield hit a 19-year high, and Japan's 10-year rose to 2.941%, above its spring peak. Brent crude settled just below $91 and WTI approached $85 after the collapse of the Iran talks. The Dow and S&P 500 each fell about 0.5% and the Nasdaq lost 0.3%. University of Michigan year-ahead inflation expectations stayed above 4% for a fifth consecutive month.

In plain termsA government bond is a loan to a country, and the "yield" is the interest that loan pays. When investors sell bonds, prices fall and yields rise, so a rising yield means lenders are demanding more to hold government debt. They are demanding more because expensive oil feeds inflation, and inflation eats the value of a fixed interest payment. This reaches ordinary life because mortgage rates and business borrowing costs track those same yields.

Why it mattersA war-driven yield spike squeezes the Federal Reserve from both sides at once: cutting rates would feed the inflation, and holding them raises what the government pays on its own debt.

Sources: CNBC · Bloomberg · TheStreet

Healthcare

AstraZeneca's Tagrisso combination extended survival in MET-driven lung cancer

AstraZeneca and partner HUTCHMED said Monday that the Phase III SAFFRON trial met both primary endpoints: Tagrisso (osimertinib) plus Orpathys (savolitinib) delivered statistically significant and clinically meaningful improvements in progression-free and overall survival versus doublet platinum-based chemotherapy. Patients had EGFR-mutated non-small cell lung cancer with high MET overexpression or amplification that had progressed on prior Tagrisso treatment. The companies said it is the first Phase III study to show both survival benefits in that setting; detailed data will be presented at a future medical meeting. The result arrives days after AstraZeneca discontinued its Phase III volrustomig lung-cancer trial for futility.

In plain termsLung tumours driven by a faulty EGFR gene are treated with Tagrisso, a pill that blocks that fault. The tumours usually learn to route around it, and the most common escape route is a second gene called MET. This trial paired Tagrisso with a second pill that blocks MET, and patients lived longer than those given chemotherapy - the standard next step once Tagrisso stops working. "Overall survival" is the hard endpoint here: it means people lived longer, not just that scans looked better. Only the top-line result is out; the actual numbers have not been published.

Why it mattersBeating chemotherapy on survival after targeted therapy fails makes MET testing at progression close to mandatory, which changes what oncologists order rather than only what they prescribe.

Sources: AstraZeneca · OncLive · Pharmacy Times

Wildcard - AI Industry

OpenAI launched a teen version of ChatGPT and will assign users to it by guessing their age

OpenAI launched ChatGPT for Teens on Tuesday, a separate version of the chatbot for users aged 13 to 17. Accounts that state a teen age - and accounts its new age-prediction system estimates belong to someone under 18 - are placed into it automatically. The teen mode blocks romantic language and terms of endearment, instructs the model not to suggest it has feelings or consciousness, adds break reminders, and warns before sensitive image uploads. The prediction system draws on signals including the topics a user discusses, the time of day they log on, and how long the account has existed. Rollout is global, with the EU following in coming weeks.

In plain termsChatGPT is the chatbot made by OpenAI that answers questions in conversation. OpenAI now guesses how old you are from clues like what you ask about, when you log on, and how long you have had the account - and if it guesses under 18, it switches you to a stricter version. That version will not flirt, will not claim to have feelings, and nudges you to take breaks. The guessing is the notable part: nobody shows ID, so some adults will be misfiled as teens and some teens will slip through.

Why it mattersAge inference rather than age verification is now the default control on a mainstream AI product, so the accuracy of a statistical guess - not a document - decides what hundreds of millions of users are allowed to see.

Sources: Axios · The Next Web · OpenAI

Wildcard - AI Industry

Etched raised $700 million at a $21 billion valuation and shipped its first chips to a customer

Etched said Tuesday it raised $700 million at a $21 billion valuation in a round led by Jane Street, with Kleiner Perkins, Sequoia, Andreessen Horowitz, Tiger Global, Bain Capital Ventures and Blackstone participating. The company was valued at $5 billion in December and $10.3 billion in a $300 million round in July - roughly a doubling within a month. Etched also named Jane Street as its first customer and said it shipped its first rack to the quantitative trading firm in July, which is deploying the hardware in live workloads. Jane Street said it tested the chip and was pleased with early results.

In plain termsAlmost all AI today runs on Nvidia's general-purpose chips, which can run any kind of program. Etched builds a chip that can only run one AI design - the "transformer" architecture behind chatbots - trading flexibility for speed. That bet was theoretical until now, and the real news is not the money but that a demanding customer took delivery of physical hardware and is running it. Note that Jane Street both led the funding round and is the customer, so its endorsement is not an independent one.

Why it mattersA single-purpose chip actually running production workloads is the first hard evidence that Nvidia's flexibility premium can be undercut rather than merely criticized.

Sources: TechCrunch · GlobeNewswire (company release)

Wildcard - International Affairs

Drones hit the Iraqi Kurdish prime minister's office; the KRG blames Iran, which denies it

Two explosive-laden drones struck the private office of Kurdistan Regional Government Prime Minister Masrour Barzani and the home of the region's intelligence chief in Erbil province's Pirmam district at about 00:28 on Monday. The KRG's security agency said they were Hadid-110 models launched from Iranian territory. No casualties were reported and no group claimed responsibility. Barzani condemned the strikes as "reckless and unacceptable" and "a dangerous escalation and a direct threat to the security and stability of the Kurdistan Region." A senior Iranian source denied any involvement and dismissed the incident as a "false flag" operation.

In plain termsThe Kurdistan Region is a semi-autonomous part of northern Iraq with its own government and security forces, sitting between Iran and the US-aligned world. Overnight, someone flew two bomb-carrying drones at its prime minister's office and its spy chief's house. The Kurdish security service says they came from Iran; Iran says it did nothing and the attack was staged to make it look guilty. Nobody was hurt, nobody claimed it, and neither account has been independently verified - so both remain claims.

Why it mattersIf the launch point is confirmed, the US-Iran fight has spread onto Iraqi soil and onto a partner government Washington depends on - a widening that no expired ceasefire deadline was containing.

Wildcard - Economics

US housing starts fell 12.4% in July while building permits rose

The Census Bureau and the Department of Housing and Urban Development reported Tuesday that privately owned housing starts ran at a seasonally adjusted annual rate of 1,239,000 in July, 12.4% below June's revised 1,415,000. Building permits moved the opposite way, rising 5.0% to an annual rate of 1,443,000. Single-family authorizations were up 2.5% to 894,000, and authorizations in buildings with five or more units came in at 490,000.

In plain terms"Housing starts" counts homes where builders actually broke ground. "Permits" counts homes they have been approved to build later. Starts dropped sharply while permits rose, which means builders still intend to build but paused actually digging. That gap usually reflects the cost of money rather than a lack of buyers: construction loans are priced off the same yields that jumped this month. One month of housing data is noisy and gets revised, so this is a signal, not a verdict.

Why it mattersPermits rising while starts fall is the clearest sign so far that the bond selloff is reaching the real economy and not just portfolios.

Sources: US Census Bureau / HUD · Trading Economics

Tech

Twenty-nine states opened their trial against Meta over harm to young users

Opening statements began Tuesday in federal court in Oakland in the suit brought in 2023 by 29 state attorneys general alleging Meta deliberately designed Instagram and Facebook to addict young people, misled the public about the risks, and collected children's data in violation of the federal Children's Online Privacy Protection Act. Attorneys for Colorado, California, New Jersey and Kentucky are leading the bipartisan group. US District Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers will decide the case; an eight-person jury is serving expressly in an advisory capacity. The states are seeking penalties that could in theory total as much as $1.4 trillion, plus changes to how the apps operate.

In plain termsTwenty-nine US states are suing Meta together, arguing its apps were built to keep teenagers hooked and that the company knew and said otherwise. COPPA is a federal law limiting what companies can collect about children under 13. The unusual part is the jury: normally jurors decide the outcome, but these eight only advise, and the judge alone rules. The $1.4 trillion figure is a theoretical ceiling from stacking a penalty per violation, not a realistic payout.

Why it mattersThe trial puts Meta's own internal research on teen harm in front of a judge under oath, which is a larger risk to the company than the headline penalty number.

Sources: U.S. News (AP) · Reuters · CNN

Trump News

The Supreme Court denied Trump's rehearing petition, making the E. Jean Carroll judgment final

The Supreme Court on Monday denied Trump's petition for rehearing in Trump v. Carroll, No. 25-573, without comment, weeks after it declined to take the case. The order leaves intact the 2023 Manhattan federal jury verdict finding him liable for sexually abusing writer E. Jean Carroll in the mid-1990s and for later defaming her, along with the roughly $5 million judgment. Carroll's attorney Roberta Kaplan said the order leaves Trump no remaining avenue to challenge the verdict. Reporting on the order notes the Court has not granted a rehearing petition since the 1960s.

In plain termsIn American courts, being "liable" is the civil version of losing: a jury decided it was more likely than not that something happened and ordered money paid. It is not a criminal conviction. Trump had already lost his appeal, and asking the Supreme Court to rehear a case it declined is close to a formality, since the Court has granted no such request in about sixty years. Monday's one-line order means the money judgment is final and the case is over.

Why it mattersThe finding is now permanently fixed in the public record with no procedural route left to unsettle it.

Sources: Supreme Court docket 25-573 · TIME · Forbes
